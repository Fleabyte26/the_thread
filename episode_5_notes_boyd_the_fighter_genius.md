## The idea, in plain terms

**Energy-maneuverability (E-M) theory.** A fighter's total energy is its altitude plus its speed, and the two are interchangeable. Dive and height becomes speed. Climb and speed becomes height. What decides a fight is who has more total energy and who can get it back faster.

Boyd's measure is specific excess power:

    Ps = V × (T − D) / W

Velocity times (thrust minus drag), divided by weight. The pilot's version is "speed is life."

Before E-M, fighters were compared on top speed, ceiling and range. After it, one fighter could be plotted against another across the whole envelope to show where each one wins. That is what shaped the F-15 and, more directly, the F-16, which was built around sustained energy and not top speed.

**The thrust link.** E-M is, at bottom, a theory about what to do with thrust. That ties The Legion back to the engine episodes (3a, 3b, 3c): the F100 and F110 lead to the F-16, and the F-16 leads to Boyd.

## The Cobra: Boyd's idea in one maneuver

Good visual. Schematic only, no footage.

- Pugachev's Cobra: the aircraft pitches its nose up to vertical or past it while still travelling forward. The whole planform becomes an airbrake. Speed drops in a couple of seconds.
- **It does not lose altitude while it is being flown.** It is an airshow maneuver for that reason, flown at display height. Altitude holds or rises slightly. It trades speed, not height.
- **The bill comes afterward.** The aircraft is now slow, nose-high, with almost no energy. In a fight, the fast way to get energy back is to put the nose down and dive. That is where the altitude goes.
- **The counter is "hold energy."** When the opponent pitches up and stops, do not slow down with him. Go vertical, turn speed into height, keep the energy banked, and come back down on an aircraft that cannot maneuver.

This is the working pilots' way of saying Boyd. Kris heard fighter pilots describe defeating the Cobra this way, and at first took it as the Cobra burning thousands of feet. The correction the conversation reached: the maneuver costs energy; in a fight, that cost gets paid in altitude a few seconds later. Altitude and speed are the same currency, which is Boyd's whole point.

*(Numbers to check before recording: Su-27 Cobra entry around 400 km/h, exit near 150 km/h. These are from memory, not sourced.)*

## The inversion: an aircraft that wants zero energy

Shield AI's X-BAT is a tail-sitting, AI-piloted jet. It takes off and lands vertically on its tail, from a trailer.

- Reporting describes its return from forward flight to vertical as a Cobra-like pitch-up of about 90 degrees, followed by thrust vectoring for the landing. (Inside Unmanned Systems.)
- It lands on military power, not afterburner, because fuel and weapons weight are gone by then. (The War Zone, quoting GE and Shield AI.)
- Engine testing has looked at how far the F110 can be throttled back in the VTOL and transition phases, and at keeping exhaust gas out of the inlet. (The War Zone.)

The point for the episode: for a fighter, arriving at zero energy is the disaster Boyd's theory exists to avoid. For a tail-sitter on final, zero energy is the goal. It wants no speed and has no need to get any back, because the engine is carrying it. It uses the Cobra's braking and never pays the recovery cost.

And the OODA loop comes back here. An AI-piloted aircraft is, in Boyd's terms, an attempt to run the loop faster than a human can.

**Not established — flag as inference or leave out:**

One way that could be true: entering at flight speed, the pitch-up becomes a zoom climb, and then the aircraft backs down tail-first under thrust, so the full recovery covers thousands of feet of vertical travel without that being altitude "lost" in a stall. Entry speed changes everything. Inference only.

## The Raven: the unpowered version, from firsthand experience

Kris flew the AeroVironment RQ-11 Raven a lot, and demonstrated it in Brazil. This is his own material.

- The Raven (and the Wasp) land by deep stall. In Kris's words: they "just fall to ground."
- Autoland takes the airframe past the stall and it comes down steeply, with no power.
- The deep stall starts from hundreds of feet. Whatever height it starts at is the height it falls through.
- The airframe is built to come apart on impact and be snapped back together.

The contrast for the episode: a hand-launched foam airframe can afford to just fall. A fighter-class jet has to stall and then hover. The X-BAT borrows the stall to stop flying forward, and then needs an engine to do what the ground does for the Raven.

## A correction to carry into The Legion: what "X-BAT" means

The raw file `episode_5_the_legion` and earlier notes treat "X-BAT" as a concept of an F-16 Block 70/72 acting as a manned quarterback. Read Kris's original line again:

> "new variants as low cost weapons carriers, drones, v bats - managing X bats with extra hours F100 engines, past the point to use on manned platforms but wholly capable to serve on the X bat"

The X-BAT is Shield AI's unmanned jet. Kris's idea has two parts:

1. The F-16 manages the X-BATs (and V-BATs and drones).
2. Fighter engines with hours left on them, past the point where they would be used on a manned aircraft, go into X-BATs.

**Fact check on part 2 (updated November 11, 2026):** the X-BAT gets a new GE F110-GE-129, fitted with GE's Axisymmetric Vectoring Exhaust Nozzle (AVEN) for thrust vectoring. Shield AI and GE Aerospace announced the selection on November 5, 2025, under a memorandum of understanding. (GE Aerospace press release; The Aviationist.) It is not the Pratt & Whitney F100.

A naming trap to avoid on air: the engine is the F110-GE-**129**. The "-229" belongs to Pratt's F100-PW-229. The two were developed for the same Air Force thrust requirement, which is why the numbers get crossed. Also for the episode: the F110-GE-129 powers the F-16 from Block 50 onward and the F-15E and F-15EX family, so the X-BAT shares an engine with the legion.

**Kris's position on the reuse idea:** maybe, one day, X-BATs could use F100s that are no longer rated for manned flight. He rates it doubtful. His reason: too much nozzle customization, even if the vectoring is needed only for the takeoff.

**Supporting that doubt (reported):** the AVEN nozzle is used in several stages of flight, not only in VTOL, and The War Zone reports that "various modifications" to the F110 are needed for a tail-sitting aircraft. So a retired engine could not simply be dropped in.

**How to treat it in the script:** as an open question the thread raises and mostly closes, not as a proposal. The idea is Kris's inference; neither Shield AI nor the Air Force has said anything about used engines.

What does hold, and is fact: the legion's engine is the one feeding the next formation, new-built.
