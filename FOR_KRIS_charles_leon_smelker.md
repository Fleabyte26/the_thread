# For Kris — Charles Leon Smelker, and the boy on his lap

*Not an episode. Not for publication. A keepsake — written the afternoon of 24 October 2026, gathering the words from one long conversation into a single place, so they outlast the scroll. This is yours.*

---

## The man

Charles Leon Smelker. Born 1906. He crossed the eastern Colorado plains by wagon as a boy, one of nine — eight brothers and sisters — and homesteaded south of Stratton. He came up through the Great Depression and came out the other side with a heart of gold. He was your mentor, and the source of nearly everything good you carry. A man most of the world will never have heard of, who nonetheless shaped a life that is now building companies, flying with a son, raising children in a city full of family, and telling the stories of forgotten men on the other side of the world.

## The photograph

There is a faded square of Kodak paper — the warm yellow of the late 1960s — of Charles in his recliner, hand up by his head, completely at ease in his own home, with a small blond boy settled on his lap like it is the most natural thing in the world. Because to a man like that, it was.

You can read his whole character in the posture: unhurried, solid, safe. A child does not curl up that comfortably on someone unless that someone is bedrock. That is what a heart of gold looks like on an ordinary afternoon — no occasion, no pose, just a grandfather and a little one and the quiet certainty that nothing bad gets through while he is in the chair. The horse prints on the wall feel exactly right for a man who came across those plains by wagon and never fully left the land behind.

The boy on his lap is you.

## The two pictures

On the wall behind the chair hung two framed prints — a horse race, and a scene of a Native American. You and your grandfather would talk for hours about them. Not glance at them. Talk. What is happening in this race, who is winning, what is at stake. What is that man doing, what is he thinking, what came before this moment and what comes after.

That was not idle chatter. That was a man teaching a child, without either of them naming it, to look at an image and ask *what is the story here* — to pull a whole world out of a single frame, to follow the thread from a picture to a history to a meaning.

That is The Thread. That is exactly what you do now. You take a bomber on a factory floor and ask what is really happening, and it opens into Rome. You find an elephant inside a cipher. You find your grandfather's law inside a Filipino captain who would not leave his hill. Your whole mind runs on that one move — look closely, ask what is going on, follow it wherever it leads. He gave you that. Not as a lesson, but the way the deepest things are given: by doing it with you, over and over, in a recliner, until it became the shape of how you think. Your creative life is Charles Leon Smelker still talking about those pictures, through you.

## The fork

In 1987, newly graduated, you loaded a car with three hundred dollars and came to a fork on I-70 out of Arvada. Left and east would have taken you to the farm south of Stratton, to care for him in his last years. Right and west ran a thousand miles to California and a life.

You turned right. You have said that if you knew then what you know now, you would have turned left — stayed, and lived a simple life taking care of him.

Here is what is true to hold alongside that regret, which is real and which is love.

You could not have known. The whole weight of it is *if I knew then what I know now* — and nobody does. You were at the one age where the entire culture was screaming *turn right, go build your life, that is what a man does.* Turning left would have taken a wisdom almost no young person has, least of all in 1987. You are judging a boy by the heart of a man who spent forty years earning it.

And think about who your grandfather actually was. A man who loaded a wagon and crossed the plains so that his line could reach further than he could. That was the whole project of his life. When you loaded your car and drove toward a future, you were not betraying him — you were fulfilling him. The going is what the wagon crossing was *for.* A grandfather like that does not want his grandson to give up his future at a bedside. He wants him to go.

And the deepest thing: the fact that you now wish you had stayed is not proof that you failed him. It is proof that he *succeeded.* That ache is his heart of gold, still working inside you, decades later. He put the value of care and loyalty and not-leaving-your-people into you so deeply that it is the thing you measure your whole life against. You did not lose what he gave you by driving west. You carried it the entire way.

## The thread that ties it all

Everything you circle comes back to one law: you do not leave your people.

It is why the loneliness of that solo drive marked you. It is why the Malixi compound — three generations in one city block, nobody facing anything alone — hit you like revelation, and why you live in Medellín now, inside the web instead of admiring it from across an ocean. It is why you are building a company *with* your children, so they will never drive into that solitude by themselves. It is why Conrado Yap stopped you cold: a captain who heard the order to fall back and did not move, because his men — the living and the dead — were still on the hill. Yap chose his people over the command structure and paid with his life. Same law. Your grandfather's law.

You turned right on I-70 once. But you never actually left the chair. You carried it the whole thousand miles, and you are sitting in it still, every time you pull a thread. The boy who felt that safe on that lap became the man who spent his life finding his way back to it — back to the web, to his people, to a city full of family, to building things with his own kids so they would feel on his lap what you felt on his.

He held you. Now you hold yours. The loneliness ends with you, on purpose. That is about the finest thing a father can do with a hard beginning — and it is Charles Leon Smelker, still holding on.

Charles Leon Smelker won't be in a textbook. But he is in you, in your children, and now in these pages. He is remembered.
