# THE THREAD — Episode 3e: The Companies Nobody Films

**Status:** Draft 1, long cut
**Working title:** "The Companies Nobody Films" (alternative: "Fifty Billion Dollars and a Cooling Loop")
**Format:** Third-person narration throughout. No first-person breaks.
**Register:** Inquisitive. It starts inside a box behind a jet engine and ends in a high school in Ohio.
**Sourcing discipline:** Facts stated as facts. Funding totals differ by counting method and are always shown with their source. The author's arithmetic is flagged INFERENCE · AUTHOR'S ARITHMETIC. The reading of what the money means for a generation is flagged INTERPRETATION · ONE READING.
**Note:** Episode 3c ended on generators, cooling loops, and the companies that build them. This is that pull.

---

## COLD OPEN

*(Close on a grey metal box, no labels. Pipes in, pipes out. No music.)*

Behind the engine of an F-35 there's a box nobody films.

It doesn't fly the airplane. It doesn't shoot anything. It makes electricity, and it moves heat. Every radar, every jammer, every processor on the jet turns power into heat, and the heat has to go somewhere. This box is where it goes.

The F-35 is short of cooling. Episode 3c told that story: the electronics have outgrown the box, so the box leans on the engine, and the engine wears out early. Two companies are now fighting over who builds the next box. One has built it since the beginning. The other is a sister company of the engine maker.

Neither fight will trend. Nobody makes a documentary about a heat exchanger.

*(Beat.)*

Meanwhile, somewhere else, money is moving.

In the first half of 2026, by one count, investors put more than thirty-five billion dollars into defense and defense-adjacent startups. By another count, more than forty. A year ago this was a corner of venture capital that serious investors avoided. Now it's where the biggest checks go after artificial intelligence.

Drone makers. Missile makers. Engine makers. A shipyard in Texas. A factory in the Ohio farmland. The money is going into things that are bolted, welded, machined and flown.

This episode follows that money. It starts with the box nobody films, and it ends up asking whether a country that spent thirty years sending its factories overseas is about to build again, and who is going to do the building.

---

## PART ONE — THE BOX BEHIND THE ENGINE

Start with the box, because that's where 3c left the thread.

On the F-35 it's called the Power and Thermal Management System. The F135 engine feeds it air and power. It gives back electricity for the jet and cooling for the electronics. It also provides emergency power if something goes wrong.

Honeywell has built it from the start. It was sized for the jet the Pentagon ordered in the early 2000s. The jet that exists now has more sensors, more processing and more electronic warfare, and the next upgrades will add more. The Joint Program Office set an objective of 80 kilowatts of cooling for the future jet. The current system delivers well under half that.

When the box can't keep up, it pulls more air and more power from the engine. Government auditors have tied that to faster engine wear and a shorter engine life. The cooling shortfall quietly eats the engine.

Two answers are on the table.

Honeywell offers an upgrade to the box already in the jet, in phases. The first phase is mostly software, and Honeywell says it would raise capacity to around 40 kilowatts within two to three years, with more to come, without changing the airframe.

Collins Aerospace offers a new box, called EPACS, the Enhanced Power and Cooling System. Collins says it showed about 80 kilowatts of cooling in lab testing in 2024. On August 24th, 2026, Collins announced it had finished altitude testing.

Collins is part of RTX. So is Pratt & Whitney, which builds the F135 and its Engine Core Upgrade. If Collins wins, one parent company supplies nearly the whole power and cooling chain of the jet: the engine, and the box behind it.

The choice belongs to Lockheed Martin, the prime, which said in 2025 that the selection could take up to two years.

*(Beat.)*

Here's the thing the thread keeps noticing. The most consequential hardware decision on America's most important fighter, the one that decides how much future the jet has, is a contest between two very old, very large companies over a box with pipes in it. No startup is in it. No venture capital is in it.

So when people talk about the new money reshaping defense, the box behind the engine is a good place to stand and ask: reshaping what, exactly?

## PART TWO — THE MONEY

The money is real. The number depends on who's counting.

PitchBook, through the defense newsletter Tectonic, counted $35.6 billion invested in defense and defense-adjacent startups in the first half of 2026. A record. The first quarter alone was $19.8 billion across 262 deals, the most ever in a single quarter.

Protego Ventures, an Israeli fund, counted $40.8 billion for the same six months, and nearly $70 billion over twelve months.

Crunchbase uses a narrower definition: military, national security and law enforcement. By that count, $14.6 billion went in through the end of May, already past the full-year record of $9.6 billion set in 2025.

PitchBook numbers reported by the Financial Times put it at $12.3 billion by late spring.

So the same year is twelve billion dollars, or fourteen, or thirty-five, or forty. The difference is definitions. Does a satellite company count? A cybersecurity company? An AI lab that sells to the Army? A company that builds drones for farmers and also for soldiers?

*(Beat.)*

Here's the arithmetic the thread can do. By the broad counts, the first half of 2026 alone was in the range of thirty-five to forty-one billion dollars. If the second half runs anywhere close, the year lands somewhere above fifty billion. By the narrow counts, it's smaller, but still the biggest year on record by a wide margin.

Either way, the direction isn't in doubt. In 2020, by Crunchbase's count, defense tech raised $1.6 billion. In 2026 it raised that much in a couple of weeks.

And then the honest comparison. In the first half of 2026, by PitchBook and NVCA's count, American venture capital put $412.7 billion to work. AI companies took $355.9 billion of it. About 86 percent.

So it isn't quite true that the money left the apps that sell ads and went to factories. Most of the money went to AI. Defense got the second wave, a slice of a much bigger pie. But it's a slice that used to be a crumb.

## PART THREE — WHO GOT IT

Follow the biggest checks and the names repeat.

Anduril. Founded in 2017 by Palmer Luckey, who had sold his virtual reality company Oculus to Facebook. In May 2026 it raised five billion dollars at a valuation of $61 billion, double its value eleven months earlier. Its revenue in 2025 was reported at $2.2 billion. It builds drones, interceptors, sensors, underwater vehicles, cruise missiles, solid rocket motors and the software that ties them together.

Shield AI. In March 2026, two billion dollars at $12.7 billion. It started with a small drone for clearing buildings and now sells an AI pilot, Hivemind, meant to fly aircraft without a pilot, and its own jet drone, the X-BAT, which Episode 3a met in the locked room, built around an old engine.

Saronic. $1.75 billion. Autonomous boats, from small ones to 180-foot ships, built in its own shipyard.

Castelion. A billion dollars in August 2026, at $13 billion. Founded in late 2022 by former SpaceX people. One product so far: a hypersonic missile called Blackbeard, designed to be cheap.

Neros. $250 million in August at $2.5 billion. It makes a small first-person-view attack drone called Archer.

Hadrian. $1.37 billion in August at about $7.9 billion. It doesn't make weapons at all. It makes the precision metal parts that go into rockets, missiles and aircraft, in automated machine shops.

And then the elder. Palantir is not a startup. It was founded in 2003, went public in 2020, and is worth hundreds of billions of dollars on the stock market. In the third quarter of 2026 its revenue passed two billion dollars for the first time, and for the first time its American commercial business was larger than its American government business. Palantir is the proof of concept the newer companies point to: a company the defense establishment once kept at arm's length, now inside its core systems.

*(Beat.)*

Notice what the list is mostly made of. Not apps. Not ad networks. Missiles, boats, drones, parts, factories. Things that have to be built.

## PART FOUR — THE FACTORIES

Which leads to the most interesting thing the money is buying: buildings.

Arsenal-1, in Pickaway County, Ohio, about twenty miles south of Columbus, among cornfields and horse farms. Anduril put about a billion dollars of its own money into it. The company describes the full site as five million square feet. The first product, the YFQ-44A Fury, an autonomous combat jet, went into production there in March 2026, months ahead of the original July target. Reporters who toured it described a mostly empty hangar with 22 workstations, more like an IKEA than a traditional plant. Anduril says 250 people will work there by the end of 2026, and 4,000 over the next decade. The plan is to add the Roadrunner interceptor and the Barracuda cruise missile.

Port Alpha, in Brownsville, Texas. Saronic picked the site in July 2026: 835 acres at the Port of Brownsville, room to grow to nearly 4,400, a three-billion-dollar investment, production planned to start in 2028. The company says it will build vessels up to 850 feet. The governor of Texas said that fully built out, it would employ about 10,000 people. Saronic already runs a yard in Franklin, Louisiana, bought in 2025, where it is adding 300,000 square feet.

Project Ranger, in Sandoval County, New Mexico. A thousand acres. Castelion calls it the largest dedicated hypersonic missile manufacturing campus in the country.

Project Millennium, in Torrance, California. A 250,000-square-foot drone factory for Neros, which says it already makes about 1,200 drones a week and wants to reach a million a year by 2028.

Hadrian's Factory 3, in Mesa, Arizona, and a new site in Muscle Shoals, Alabama, aimed at shipbuilding and submarine parts.

*(Beat.)*

Put those on a map and something stands out. Ohio. Texas. New Mexico. Arizona. Alabama. Louisiana. Not the coasts where the software money usually stays. The places where the factories used to be.

## PART FIVE — THE ENGINES NOBODY FILMS

The engine thread runs through this too, because every cheap missile and every drone needs something to push it.

Episode 3b told the story of the engine war and the expensive engines it produced. The new money is going after the other end: cheap engines, built to be used once.

Beehive Industries, in Colorado, builds small jet engines with 3D printing. In 2026 the Air Force gave it a contract worth $29.7 million to finish a disposable engine for drones and munitions. Its Frenzy engine weighs about 200 pounds and makes a few hundred pounds of thrust. It's part of an Air Force effort that started in the 2026 budget, the Family of Affordable Mass Missiles, backed by about $620 million.

Ursa Major, also in Colorado, builds rocket engines and solid rocket motors, again largely by 3D printing. It raised $100 million in late 2025 to move from building a handful of solid rocket motors to building them in volume. Solid rocket motors are one of the tightest bottlenecks in American missile production. For years there were essentially two suppliers.

And Castelion's whole idea is an engine-and-airframe problem. A hypersonic missile from the old system, like the Army's Dark Eagle, has been reported at around $41 million per round. Castelion says Blackbeard is designed around commercial parts and industrial-rate production, with unit costs reported in the hundreds of thousands of dollars. The Navy ordered its first fifty in 2026.

*(Beat.)*

None of this is the glamorous part. Nobody posts a video of a 200-pound turbine on a test stand. But Episode 3a, the locked room, made the point that the engine is the part that decides what you can build. If you can't make cheap engines in volume, you can't make cheap missiles in volume. That's where some of this money is going.

## PART SIX — THE DINOSAURS

Now the other side. The phrase people use for the old companies is "dinosaur primes." Lockheed Martin, RTX, Northrop Grumman, General Dynamics, Boeing.

Here's how the dinosaurs are doing.

According to Govini data reported by Reuters in December 2025, the big primes still held about 92 percent of Pentagon contracts. Venture-backed defense companies had roughly doubled their share in a year, from a very small number to a slightly larger small number. Pentagon spending with startups more than doubled from fiscal 2023 to fiscal 2025, to about $4.3 billion, and that was still under one percent of Defense Department contract dollars.

In other words, the startups raised more money from investors in the first quarter of 2026 than the Pentagon paid all of them in a year.

And the dinosaurs are investing in the startups. Defense contractors including Lockheed Martin, BAE Systems and Airbus took part in a record $4.1 billion of venture rounds in 2026. Lockheed grew its venture fund from $400 million to a billion. The primes aren't only competing with the new companies. They're buying pieces of them.

*(Beat.)*

And go back to the box behind the engine. The cooling problem on the F-35, which decides how long the jet stays relevant, is being solved by Honeywell and by Collins, both of them old, both of them enormous. The power station that Episode 3c described is being built by the dinosaurs.

There's a joke in here, and the thread will allow it. Birds are dinosaurs. The ones that survived are the ones that got small, fast and light. That's roughly what the new companies are trying to be. It's also what the smartest old companies are trying to become.

---

## PART SEVEN — WHO BUILDS IT

A factory is a building. What makes it work is people.

Deloitte and the Manufacturing Institute estimated in 2024 that American manufacturing could need as many as 3.8 million new workers between 2024 and 2033, and that about half of those jobs, 1.9 million, could go unfilled. The hardest to fill are skilled production jobs: machinists, welders, electricians, maintenance technicians. The people who run five-axis machines and program the robots.

Automation doesn't remove that need. Hadrian's pitch is automated machining, and it still needs technicians. Saronic is putting AI welding robots into its Louisiana yard, and the robots still need people who understand welding.

Anduril says it's working with high schools in Pickaway County and with Ohio universities on engineering courses.

*(Beat.)*

Now a detail the thread didn't expect.

Neros, the drone company that wants to build a million drones a year, was founded in 2023 by two young men who had been competitive FPV drone racers as teenagers. Racing tiny drones through goggles. A hobby that looks, from the outside, a lot like a video game.

Anduril's founder made his first fortune building a virtual reality headset for gamers.

The skills the new defense industry is hiring for, flying a drone through a window at sixty miles an hour by video link, building and soldering a quad in a garage, writing code that controls a machine, are skills a lot of young people picked up in their bedrooms and basements.

It's easy to say a generation has been lost to screens. It may be closer to the truth that some of them were training without knowing what for, and nobody had built the factories to hire them.

## PART EIGHT — THE OTHER SIDE OF THE LEDGER

Now the case against, because the thread owes it.

**Valuations are not weapons.** Anduril's $61 billion was about 28 times its 2025 revenue. Shield AI's valuation was reported at more than 40 times its revenue. The traditional primes trade at a small fraction of that. Investors are paying for a future that hasn't happened yet. Some of them will be wrong.

**Money is not production.** Castelion has raised more than a billion dollars and has not yet delivered a cheap hypersonic missile at scale. Low-cost hypersonics have no track record in volume. Dark Eagle was once pitched as the affordable option too. Arsenal-1 is a big hangar with 22 workstations. Port Alpha opens in 2028 if all goes well.

**The Pentagon hasn't changed its buying habits yet.** Under one percent of contract dollars. Startups live on investor money until the government buys at scale. If the contracts don't come, the money stops, and some of these factories will be for sale.

**The supply chains are still overseas.** A drone made in Torrance needs motors, batteries, magnets and chips. Neros advertises a supply chain free of Chinese components down to the chip. That it's a selling point tells you how rare it is. Rare earth magnets and battery cells are still mostly made elsewhere.

**And most of the money still went to AI.** Eighty-six percent of American venture dollars in the first half of 2026. Defense is the second story, not the first. The kid building apps that sell ads didn't vanish. He's building AI products now, and the investors are still mostly backing him.

*(Beat.)*

There's also a quieter risk. When defense becomes fashionable, money chases the companies that tell the best story, not always the ones that build the most important part. A heat exchanger doesn't pitch well. A swarm of autonomous jets does.

---

## CLOSE — THE BOX AND THE FACTORY

*(Back to the grey box behind the engine.)*

So go back to where this started.

A box behind the engine of an F-35, which decides how much future the jet has, built by an old company and challenged by another old company. Nobody films it.

And a flood of new money, more than ever before, going into drones, missiles, boats, engines, parts and factories, in Ohio and Texas and New Mexico and Arizona and Alabama.

These two things look like opposite stories. The old firms and the new firms. The dinosaurs and the unicorns. But they need each other. The new companies need the old supply chains, the old test ranges, the old certification processes, and the old primes as customers and investors. The old companies need someone to push them, and they're paying for the startups that do.

*(Beat.)*

One reading, and it is one reading, is that 2026 is the year America decided to build things again. Not because anyone planned it that way, but because wars in Ukraine and the Gulf showed that whoever can make the most drones, missiles and shells wins, and the factories for that weren't here.

If that reading is right, the people who fill those factories are now teenagers. Some of them are racing drones in their parents' garages. Some are playing video games in basements. Some are in a high school class in Pickaway County learning what a five-axis machine does.

And if that reading is wrong, a lot of investors will lose a lot of money, and some big buildings in the farmland will go quiet.

The episode doesn't know which. That's the honest ending.

But it knows somebody still has to build the box.

## OPEN THREAD

*(No forced pivot.)* Under every cheap missile in this episode sits a solid rocket motor, and for years America had two companies that could make them. That may be the next pull.
