# THE THREAD — Episode 6: Steady On

**Status:** Published November 12, 2026: https://youtu.be/jlbiP5MwWqk (14 min 0 s with the Piper voice)
**Series slot:** Thread B, the human cost and memory. Pulled from the Open Thread of Episode 5, "Faith." Built from the treatment in `episode_12_yultong_the_line_that_held.md`. (SERIES_MAP.md lists "American-Canadian Rejuvenation" as Episode 6; this episode takes the number in published order.)
**Format:** Third-person narration throughout. No first-person breaks.
**Rule for this episode (Kris, November 12, 2026):** tell the possibilities as we know them. Nobody alive knows exactly what happened on the hill. The narration never says "what happened was." It says who wrote what, sets the versions side by side, and says what they have in common.
**The thread it pulls:** Faith's daughter, Yap's daughter. The love of a child for a father who did not come home.

---

## COLD OPEN

*(A ship at sea. No music.)*

The last episode ended with a daughter. She was four when her father left for Korea, and she waited sixty-two years to bury him.

This one begins with another daughter, in another country, whose father went to the same war.

She was a small girl too. Her name is Isabelita. Her father was a captain in the Philippine Army. He left for Korea in 1950 and he did not come home. She is an old woman now. In 2018 she flew to Seoul, and the prime minister of South Korea put her father's medal in her hands. He had been dead for sixty-seven years.

*(Beat.)*

And she has said something that the first daughter might understand better than anyone. She has said that her father is still on patrol.

She means it almost exactly. There is a warship in the Philippine Navy that carries his name. It was built in South Korea. It served in the South Korean navy for thirty years. Then South Korea gave it to the Philippines, as a gift, and the Philippines painted a dead Army captain's name on the hull.

To understand why, you have to go to a hill above a village called Yultong. And you have to accept one thing before you start. Nobody agrees on exactly what happened there. Three governments wrote it down, and they wrote it three ways.

This episode will tell you all of them.

---

## PART ONE — THE MAN

His name was Conrado Yap. He was born in 1921 in Candelaria, a small town in the province of Zambales, on the west coast of Luzon.

A Filipino account of his life says he was first in his class. He finished high school in the provincial capital and went to Manila to study electrical engineering. Then he gave it up. In 1939 he entered the Philippine Military Academy.

He did not get a normal graduation. When the Pacific war began, at the end of 1941, his class was sent out early, to fight.

What came next is told in two short phrases in the record, and each of them is a whole story. He survived the Bataan Death March. And afterward he served as operations chief in the guerrilla army.

It helps to know what the first of those means. In April of 1942, the American and Filipino army on the Bataan peninsula surrendered to Japan. Tens of thousands of prisoners were marched north for some sixty-five miles, in the heat, with almost no food and almost no water. Men who fell out of the column were killed where they fell. Thousands died on that road, and most of them were Filipinos.

He was twenty-one. He walked it, and lived, and then went into the hills and kept fighting for three more years.

*(Beat.)*

So before he was twenty-five, this man had already seen what happens to soldiers who are left behind.

In 1944, in the middle of all that, he married a woman named Aurora de la Llana. They had three daughters. Isabelita was the second.

After the war he stayed in the Army. In 1949 he was sent to the United States, to the armor school at Fort Knox, to learn tanks. He came home a captain, in command of a tank company.

Hold on to that. A tank company.

---

## PART TWO — A TANK COMPANY WITH NO TANKS

In June of 1950, North Korea invaded the South, and the United Nations asked its members for soldiers.

The Philippines was in no condition to send any. The country had been wrecked by the war, only five years before. It had a rebellion of its own to fight at home. It sent them anyway. It was the first country in Asia to send combat troops to Korea, and only the third member of the United Nations to do it.

The unit was called the 10th Battalion Combat Team. About fourteen hundred men. They sailed from Manila on September 15th, 1950, and landed at Pusan four days later.

They had been organized as a motorized force, and the Americans had promised them tanks. The tanks never came. So Captain Yap's tank company became a heavy weapons company, with machine guns in place of armor. The record uses both names for it, which is why this episode does too.

There is one more detail from that first winter. Most of these men had never seen snow. They went north into the Korean cold, and the winter clothing they were promised did not come either.

They found a fight quickly all the same. On November 11th, 1950, near a place called Miudong, the battalion met two North Korean battalions and beat them. At home it is remembered as the first battle the Philippines ever won on foreign soil. A country that had been somebody's colony for more than three hundred years, and its own master for four, had sent its army abroad under its own flag, and won.

*(Beat.)*

By April of 1951 the battalion was down to about nine hundred men. It held a place in the middle of the line, north of a town called Yeoncheon. On one side of it was a brigade from Turkey. On the other was an American regiment from Puerto Rico. Filipinos, Turks and Puerto Ricans, shoulder to shoulder on a Korean hillside. That was the line.

On the night of April 22nd, the Chinese army began its spring offensive, and it came straight at that line.

How many came at the Filipinos? Here is the first place the accounts divide, and they divide wildly. Philippine accounts say forty thousand. The Chinese account says two battalions, six hundred to a thousand men. Those two numbers are forty to one apart. The two sides do not even agree on which Chinese division it was.

This episode cannot settle that, and it will not pretend to. Take the smallest number. Nine hundred men, at night, cut into pieces, against a force their own size that had chosen the time and the place. That is still a fight.

---

## PART THREE — THE NIGHT

The Turkish brigade was hit first, at about eight in the evening. The Filipinos were hit from about eleven.

It came in waves, and within hours the battalion was no longer one battalion. Each company was fighting alone, in the dark, with no contact with the others. It was bad enough that the men who were not supposed to fight had to. The drivers. The typists. The clerks. The medics. The cooks. And the chaplain.

*(Beat.)*

On a hill above the village of Yultong, farther forward than anyone else, was a young first lieutenant named Jose Artiaga Junior. He had come out of the same academy as Yap, one class behind him.

Here the record divides again, in a smaller way. The histories of the battle say Artiaga commanded one of the battalion's rifle companies. The two medal citations, the Philippine one and the American one, say he led a platoon in Yap's own company. If the citations are right, then the men on that hill were Yap's own men.

What Artiaga did is not in dispute. His platoon was cut off from everyone. It was attacked again and again by a larger force, under machine guns, mortars and artillery. He moved among his men, placing them and steadying them. They threw back attack after attack. And while he was directing their fire, he was hit, and he died there.

By three in the morning, part of the forward position had been pushed off its ground. After four hours of fighting, the Chinese had reached the company that was being held in reserve, well behind the line.

The American citation adds one sentence. His men held their position until relief came.

There is a photograph, taken in Korea some time before that night. It is described as the last known picture of the two of them together. Captain Yap on the left. Lieutenant Artiaga on the right.

---

## PART FOUR — THE ORDER

Some time after midnight, with the forward hill overrun and the Chinese deep inside the battalion's position, the battalion commander gave the order to pull back.

His name was Dionisio Ojeda. He had survived the Death March too. He was not a man who frightened easily, and the order was the correct one. Every company obeyed it. Every company except one.

Now. Why did Yap's company not withdraw? There are three answers, and each one comes from a government.

The first is from the United States Army. The American citation says Yap learned that his forward platoon had been overrun, and that he led a charge to reach it even though his battalion commander had ordered a withdrawal. In that version, he was told to leave, and he refused.

The second is from the Philippine Army. Its citation uses different words. It says he acted despite an authority to withdraw, which he had already received. Not an order. A permission. In that version, he was told he could leave, and he chose not to.

The third is from South Korea, and it is the newest. When Korea honored him again this year, its account said that his company never received the withdrawal order at all, because communications had broken down. In that version, there was no refusal. There was only a captain, out of contact, deciding by himself what to do about the men on the hill.

*(Beat.)*

Three versions. Ordered and refused. Permitted and declined. Or never told.

Look at what they share. In all three, he knows his men are up there. In all three, nobody is making him go. And in all three, he goes.

---

## PART FIVE — THE HILL

He had already been up and down his own line that night. The American citation says he crawled from foxhole to foxhole, under fire, steadying his men. The Philippine one says he first tried to reinforce the trapped platoon and fought for an hour without success.

Then he counterattacked.

What the attack achieved is told in three ways, and they do not contradict each other. The American citation says his company retook the hill, brought out the wounded, and rescued the cut-off platoon. The Korean history says they brought back a squad of survivors. The Philippine citation says he recovered the body of Lieutenant Artiaga, and the bodies of three other soldiers.

The living, and the dead. He went back for both.

*(Beat.)*

And then he was killed. There are four accounts of that.

The Philippine citation says that after recovering the bodies he went on, and attacked an enemy position about three hundred yards farther up, and was shot.

The American citation says he saw a wounded soldier lying on the slope, started toward him, and was cut down by a burst of fire.

A third account says he was hit by machine-gun fire while he was counting his men and directing them back.

The Korean account says he was mortally wounded while rescuing survivors and recovering his comrades.

Four versions. In not one of them is he moving away.

There is one more thing, and it comes from a Filipino war correspondent named Juan Villasanta, who was with the battalion in Korea. He reported the captain's last words. They were the motto of his company. Two words.

Steady on.

*(Beat.)*

This is commentary, not record, but it is hard not to think of it. The oldest war story in the Western world turns on the same act. In the Iliad, men fight and die for most of a day over the body of one dead friend, so that the enemy will not have it. Three thousand years later, on a hill in Korea, a captain from Zambales did the same thing, and nobody had to explain to him why.

---

## PART SIX — WHAT THREE COUNTRIES DID

At first light the battalion counterattacked, with its last two light tanks, and took back some of the ground. Later that morning the whole army was ordered to fall back to a new line. The Filipinos had held long enough to cover the regiment beside them as it pulled out.

The battalion's own report counted twelve dead from that night. Other accounts say fifteen. It is not a large number. The Philippines remembers every one of them.

Then the three countries did what countries do.

The Philippines gave him its highest decoration, the Medal for Valor, before the year was out. The president made the award himself.

There is something worth knowing about that president. His name was Elpidio Quirino, and it was Quirino who had decided to send the battalion to Korea. Six years earlier, in the battle for Manila, he had lost his wife and three of his children in a single day. So the man who spoke over a dead captain's medal that December knew, in the most exact way there is, what that kind of loss was.

The United States gave him the Distinguished Service Cross, its second-highest award for courage, in December of 1951. A few months later it gave the same cross to Lieutenant Artiaga. His own country gave Artiaga its Distinguished Conduct Star.

And South Korea waited. It waited sixty-seven years. Then, on July 27th, 2018, the anniversary of the armistice, it gave Captain Yap the Taegeuk, the highest class of its Order of Military Merit, an honor very few foreigners have ever received. The prime minister handed it to Isabelita.

It did not stop there. In 2019 came the ship. And in the summer of this year, South Korea named him its Korean War hero of the month.

*(Beat.)*

At home there is more. In his town of Candelaria, the twenty-third of April is a holiday. There is a library and a museum with his name. The cadets at the academy he left early eat their meals in a hall called Yap.

And in 2023, Isabelita stood up for him one more time. This medal was American, and it was not for Korea at all. It was for the other war, the first one, the march and the guerrillas. Eighty years late.

---

## CLOSE — STILL ON PATROL

Over the whole Korean War, about seven and a half thousand Filipinos served. One hundred and twelve did not come home.

One of them left three small daughters in Zambales. Of their mother, Aurora, who raised them, the public record says almost nothing. That is usual. The record keeps the men.

One of those daughters has now stood up to receive him twice in five years, once across the sea in Seoul and once at home, from two foreign governments, for two different wars.

Nobody gave her an order to do that either.

*(Beat.)*

Don Faith's daughter kept six children's books. Conrado Yap's daughter has a ship.

It was built in Korea in 1986. For thirty years it guarded the coast of the country he died for. Now it sails under the flag of the country he came from. When the Philippine Navy took it over, it needed a motto for the ship, and it did not have to look far.

The motto of the ship is the last thing he said.

Steady on.

1. Fort Knox, 1949. Taken from the earlier treatment (Wikipedia). Not re-verified in this pass.
2. "His class was sent out early." From a Filipino-language profile (Abante). One source.

9. "Very few foreigners have ever received" the Taegeuk. One Philippine news agency report gives 15 allied soldiers; not independently confirmed, so no number is spoken.
10. The president "made the award himself." The Official Gazette lists remarks by President Quirino at the award, dated December 8, 1951. The text of the remarks was not read in this pass.
11. "Twice in five years." July 2018 (Seoul, the Taegeuk) and September 2023 (Baguio, the U.S. Congressional Gold Medal) are both sourced.
12. The ship's thirty years. Commissioned into the South Korean navy in 1987, decommissioned December 2016, handed over August 5, 2019. "Thirty years" is 1987 to 2016.
13. The ship's motto. Listed as "Steady on" in the reference entry for the ship. Confirm against a Philippine Navy source.
14. The Iliad passage is commentary and is said to be commentary.
15. Open Thread. The battalion's attachment to the British 29th Brigade and the attempt to reach the Gloucestershire Regiment at the Imjin are in the battle histories. Whether that is the next episode is Kris's call; Edson's Ridge is next in the series map's valor thread.
16. The Bataan Death March paragraph (April 1942, about sixty-five miles, thousands dead, most of them Filipino) is general history, not from this episode's sources. The figures are deliberately round. "He was twenty-one" follows from his birth date.
17. "Kept fighting for three more years." The guerrilla service is sourced; its length is inferred from the dates of the occupation.
