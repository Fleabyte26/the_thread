# THE THREAD — Episode 3: The Wasted Years

**Status:** Draft 1, long cut
**Format:** Third-person narration throughout. No first-person breaks.
**Register:** Long, rambling, inquisitive. It goes where it goes. It's angrier than the first two episodes, it says so, and then it checks itself.
**Sourcing discipline:** Facts stated as facts. The arithmetic that turns waste into ships is the author's and is flagged INFERENCE on screen. The Roman reading is one reading and is flagged INTERPRETATION.
**Note:** The thread follows its own questions. This episode doesn't set up any other.

---

## COLD OPEN

*(No music. A dry dock in Pascagoula, Mississippi. Or black.)*

In January 2026, a destroyer named Zumwalt went back to sea after nearly three years in a shipyard in Mississippi.

*(Beat.)*

Three years. Not for battle damage, and not for a collision. It had been there since August 2023 having its two big guns taken out, because the guns had never worked the way they were supposed to, and four very large missile tubes put in where the guns had been. Each tube holds three hypersonic missiles. Twelve missiles in all, on a ship that was designed around guns.

The Navy once planned to build thirty-two of these ships. It built three. By the Government Accountability Office's 2018 count, the program cost about $24.5 billion, research and development included. That's roughly eight billion dollars a hull before the missile conversion, which GAO said in July 2026 would cost at least another two billion for the three ships and was running about two years behind schedule.

So the question this episode keeps asking, in different ways, all the way through, is a simple one.

What else could that money have been?

And then, more quietly, a harder question: what else could those years have been?

---

## PART ONE — THE SHIP WITH NO GUNS

*(Zumwalt: the tumblehome hull, the angular deckhouse. Bath Iron Works footage.)*

Start with what the Zumwalt was meant to be, because it wasn't a stupid idea. That's what makes it hard to watch.

The Zumwalt came out of the late 1990s and early 2000s, when the Navy was thinking about fighting close to shore and supporting troops on land. It wanted a big, stealthy ship that could stand off a coastline and drop precise, long-range shells on targets far inland, the job battleships used to do. The hull slopes inward as it rises, the reverse of almost every ship built in the last century, so it shows a radar a far smaller return than a ship its size should. It has integrated electric drive, which means the engines make electricity first and then push the ship, so the same power plant can feed weapons and sensors that haven't been invented yet. It has a crew far smaller than a ship that size would normally carry.

On paper, a glimpse of the future.

The future had a price, and the price kept rising. As it rose, the number of ships fell: thirty-two, then far fewer, then three. And with only three ships, the special long-range ammunition for the Advanced Gun System had almost no one to buy it. When a program shrinks that far, the cost of every specialized part gets spread across fewer and fewer customers until the part stops making sense. The guns that the ship was designed around never became a working weapon system. They've now been removed.

*(Beat.)*

So the Navy has three very expensive, very stealthy, electrically powered hulls, and it is turning them into something nobody drew up in 1998: hypersonic strike ships. The Conventional Prompt Strike missile, the same glide body the Army uses in its Long-Range Hypersonic Weapon, gives the Zumwalt reach out to roughly 1,725 miles, against about 63 miles for the gun.

You could argue that the ship has finally found its purpose. People do argue that. Some analysts call the 2026 sea trials a rebirth.

But look at what the rebirth costs. The whole Conventional Prompt Strike program estimate grew from $31 billion for 262 missiles in 2020 to $41 billion for 224 missiles. That's more money for fewer missiles. By April 2026, GAO expected each missile to cost about $67 million on average. USS Zumwalt finished builder's sea trials on January 21, 2026, but at-sea CPS testing is scheduled for 2027. In October 2026, the second ship, Lyndon B. Johnson, got another $99 million contract modification just for system testing and sea trials. GAO's 2025 shipbuilding review put the acquisition cost at $10.6 billion per ship.

*(On screen: 32 → 3. A ghost row of 29 hulls, fading.)*

Twenty-nine ships that were never built. The money that would have built them didn't vanish. Most of it was never spent, because the ships were cancelled. What got spent was the price of learning, at full scale and in steel, that the design was too ambitious to buy in numbers.

That distinction matters, and it's going to come back.

## PART TWO — THE FRIGATE THAT WAS SUPPOSED TO BE EASY

*(Marinette, Wisconsin. Fincantieri yard. Snow.)*

If the Zumwalt was the Navy reaching too far, the Constellation was supposed to be the Navy learning its lesson.

The idea was humble on purpose. Don't design a new ship from scratch. Take a proven European design, the Italian FREMM frigate, already in service, already at sea with other navies, and build it in Wisconsin with American systems. Pick it in 2020. Build twenty. Keep it simple. The first ship was supposed to be delivered in 2026.

It wasn't.

In April 2024, USNI News reported the program was about three years late because of shipyard backlogs and a shortage of skilled workers. In April 2025, the first ship was reported to be about ten percent complete, and the design still wasn't finalized. The ship gained weight, 759 tons by one count. Every redesign added weight, and the weight passed the margins the design could absorb. The estimate of $850 to $950 million per hull climbed past $1.1 billion.

And here's the part that should stop anyone. By the time the American changes were done, the "proven European design" had drifted a long way from the original FREMM. The ship was changed so much that it lost the one thing that made it a good idea: being proven.

On November 25, 2025, Navy Secretary John Phelan cancelled it. The first two hulls, Constellation and Congress, would be finished, largely to keep the Wisconsin shipyard and its workers going. The four other ships on contract were cancelled. Eighteen of the planned twenty came off the future fleet. In February 2026, the contract on the two survivors moved from fixed-price to cost-plus. In March, the shipbuilder was told to pause ordering new material while the two ships were reviewed again.

A Navy official explained the cancellation in a sentence that could be the subtitle of this episode. The frigate had become 80 percent of the cost of an Arleigh Burke destroyer for about 60 percent of the capability. As he put it: "You might as well build destroyers."

*(Beat.)*

A month later, in December 2025, the Navy announced a new frigate program, FF(X). Its basis is the hull of the Coast Guard's National Security Cutter, a ship already in production in an American yard.

So the lesson of the Constellation, it seems, is the lesson the Constellation was supposed to be: take something that already exists and is already being built, and don't change it too much. It took about five years and a cancelled program to learn it a second time.

## PART THREE — THE LITTLE SHIPS

*(Littoral Combat Ships pierside. Freedom and Independence variants side by side, one a monohull, one a trimaran.)*

Before the Constellation there was the ship it was supposed to replace, and that's its own story.

The Littoral Combat Ship began in the early 2000s as a bold idea: fast, small, cheap warships with swappable mission modules. Fit one for mine-hunting this month, for submarine-hunting the next. Admiral Vern Clark, the Navy's top officer at the time, hoped each one would cost about $250 million, five of them for the price of one destroyer.

The Navy didn't pick one design. It bought two completely different ships from two industry teams. The Freedom variant is a steel monohull built in Wisconsin. The Independence variant is an aluminum trimaran built in Alabama. That meant two sets of spare parts, two training pipelines and two maintenance systems, for one mission.

The mission modules lagged. The costs rose. The Freedom variant turned out to have a problem in its combining gear, the transmission that joins the engines to the propellers, which some called a class-wide defect. In service, the ships proved lightly armed and lightly armored for the kind of war the Navy began planning for in the 2010s, a fight against China in the western Pacific.

So the Navy started retiring them early. Very early.

USS Sioux City cost roughly $362 million to build and served four years and nine months, about 19 percent of a 25-year design life. That works out to roughly $76 million for every year she was in the fleet. USS Fort Worth, commissioned in 2012, was decommissioned in San Diego on July 29, 2026, after nearly fourteen years, about 56 percent of her intended life. Defense News, using Congressional Budget Office data, put the loss from decommissioning the ships early at almost $7 billion.

And with the commissioning of USS Cleveland in May 2026, the Navy finished building them. The last ship of the class joined the fleet the same year others were being retired.

*(On screen: the planned number of 55, then the ordered count, then the active count, shrinking. See production notes on conflicting figures.)*

The Navy once planned 55. Analyst Dan Grazier of the Stimson Center said in 2026 that the whole concept had failed.

*(Beat.)*

There's a version of this story that's only about bad decisions. There's another version that's about something subtler. The LCS was designed for one world, a navy policing coastlines after the Cold War, and delivered into another, a navy facing a peer. Some of the waste wasn't stupidity. It was building for yesterday's question while tomorrow's arrived.

That doesn't make the money come back. But it changes who you blame.

## PART FOUR — THE CARRIER THAT TRIED EVERYTHING AT ONCE

*(Newport News. The Ford's flight deck. The EMALS catapult track.)*

Now the biggest ship of all.

The USS Gerald R. Ford was the Navy's first new carrier design in more than four decades. It's huge, about 1,100 feet long, and it went to sea carrying 23 new or modified technologies. Among them: an electromagnetic catapult in place of steam, a new arresting gear for landing aircraft, a new radar, and advanced weapons elevators to move bombs from the magazines to the flight deck faster.

Each of those ideas has a case behind it. Together they were the problem. GAO and outside analysts made the same point for years: the Navy built the ship while many of those critical technologies were still immature, so testing and fixing them happened on a finished warship, in public, at full price.

When Congress authorized the Ford in the mid-2000s, the procurement estimate was about $10.5 billion. The final procurement cost, according to the Congressional Research Service, was $13,316.5 million. That doesn't count roughly $6 billion in research and development for the class. The last Nimitz-class carrier, USS George H.W. Bush, had been completed for roughly $6.2 billion against a cost cap of a little over six billion.

So the Ford cost roughly twice the sticker price of the carrier before it.

*(Beat.)*

And it's not over. The second ship, USS John F. Kennedy, was supposed to be delivered in July 2025. Navy budget documents moved that to March 2027, to finish certifying the advanced arresting gear and keep working on, again, the weapons elevators. GAO warned it could slip to July 2027. Kennedy's own procurement cost estimate is about $13.2 billion. The third ship, Enterprise, slipped from 2029 to 2030. The fourth, Doris Miller, is expected to cost around $15 billion.

Then the scheduling bill came due. USS Nimitz, the carrier Kennedy was meant to replace, was set to retire in 2026. With Kennedy late, the Navy faces a stretch with only ten carriers, below the eleven required by law.

About ten years separate the delivery of the first Ford in 2017 from the second in 2027.

*(Beat.)*

The Ford has a defense, and the episode should say it out loud. The Navy says the class can generate more sorties per day than a Nimitz. It has far more electrical generating capacity, the same logic as the Zumwalt, leaving room for weapons and sensors that haven't been invented yet. It's designed to run with fewer sailors over a fifty-year life, where most of a carrier's cost actually sits. If those claims hold up over decades, the first-ship pain may look different in 2060.

*(On screen, small: Navy capability claims. Recheck sortie and power figures before recording.)*

But the argument against the Ford was never that the technologies were bad. It was that putting twenty-three of them on one hull at once, all unproven, meant the whole ship waited on the slowest one. A company that did that with a product launch would be studied in business schools as a warning.

### Nimitz 11 through 14

*(On screen: two rows of four carriers. Top row, ghost Nimitz-class hulls labelled "11," "12," "13," "14." Bottom row: Ford, Kennedy, Enterprise, Doris Miller. Banner: INFERENCE · AUTHOR'S ARITHMETIC.)*

So run the experiment. The Nimitz class ended at ten ships. Suppose the Navy had just kept going, Nimitz number eleven, twelve, thirteen and fourteen, instead of Fords one through four. What would the difference have been?

First, the Fords. All of these are the Congressional Research Service's numbers, from the Navy's fiscal 2026 budget, in the dollars of the year each ship was bought:

- Gerald R. Ford, bought in 2008: $13.3 billion, final.
- John F. Kennedy, bought in 2013: $13.2 billion, estimated. Delivery slipped to 2027.
- Enterprise, bought in 2018: $14.2 billion, estimated. Delivery is July 2030.
- Doris Miller, bought in 2019: $15.2 billion, estimated. Delivery is February 2032.

That's about $56 billion for four carriers. And that's after the Navy says it saved billions by buying the last two together in a two-ship block buy.

*(Beat.)*

It's worth seeing where these numbers started. In the Navy's 2008 budget, the Ford was estimated at about $10.5 billion, Kennedy at about $9.2 billion and Enterprise at about $10.7 billion. Every one of them grew. Kennedy grew by about four billion dollars.

Now the Nimitz side. The last Nimitz, George H.W. Bush, was bought in fiscal 2001 and came in at about $6.2 billion. Four more of those at the same price is $24.8 billion. Subtract that from $56 billion and the difference is about $31 billion.

But that's not fair. A dollar in 2001 isn't a dollar in 2019, so the comparison has to be adjusted. Raise the Bush price by ordinary consumer inflation to the year each Ford was bought, and a Nimitz bought alongside the Ford in 2008 comes to about $7.5 billion. Alongside Kennedy in 2013, about $8.2 billion. Alongside Enterprise and Doris Miller, about $8.8 to $9 billion. Four inflation-adjusted Nimitz carriers come to roughly $33.4 billion.

Against about $56 billion for the four Fords, the difference is roughly $22.5 billion, before counting the several billion more in research and development spent designing the class.

*(On screen, the table:)*

| Hull | Bought | Ford cost (then-year) | Nimitz at Bush price, inflated to that year | Difference |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ford / "Nimitz 11" | FY2008 | $13.3B | ~$7.5B | ~$5.8B |
| Kennedy / "Nimitz 12" | FY2013 | $13.2B | ~$8.2B | ~$5.0B |
| Enterprise / "Nimitz 13" | FY2018 | $14.2B | ~$8.8B | ~$5.5B |
| Doris Miller / "Nimitz 14" | FY2019 | $15.2B | ~$9.0B | ~$6.3B |
| **Four ships** | | **~$56.0B** | **~$33.4B** | **~$22.5B** |

That's roughly five and a half billion dollars per ship, every ship, for four ships in a row. The Ford premium didn't shrink as the class matured. It held, and on the latest ship it's the largest of the four.

*(Beat.)*

Now the caveats, honestly, because this is exactly the kind of number that gets quoted without them:

- Consumer inflation isn't shipbuilding inflation. The cost of building warships has been rising faster than the economy as a whole, so a real Nimitz 14 bought in 2019 would likely have cost more than $9 billion. That makes the $22.5 billion gap somewhat too large.
- The Nimitz design dates to the 1960s. Building four more would have meant updating it anyway, at some cost.
- The Navy's case for the Ford is over a fifty-year life: more sorties, much more electrical power, and smaller crews than a Nimitz. If those savings show up over decades, part of the gap gets paid back.
- What can't be paid back is the schedule. Nimitz 12 wouldn't have waited for advanced weapons elevators.

So call it twenty billion dollars, give or take, for the decision to put everything new on one class at once. That's about seven Flight III destroyers, or four Virginia-class submarines, out of the carrier line alone.

## PART FIVE — THE SUBMARINE THAT WAS TOO GOOD

*(Groton, Connecticut. Electric Boat. Seawolf at sea, black and enormous.)*

This one is complicated, and it's where the episode's anger starts to wobble.

The Seawolf began in 1983 as the replacement for the Los Angeles-class attack submarine, built to hunt the quietest Soviet submarines of the late Cold War. It was faster, deeper-diving, quieter, and carried more weapons than anything before it. In July 1990, GAO told Congress the Navy planned to buy 29 of them by the end of the decade for about $44 billion.

Then the Soviet Union ended.

The plan dropped to twelve in January 1991. On January 29, 1992, the first Bush administration cancelled everything beyond the lead ship. Congress pushed back and restored a second boat. Later a third was built, Jimmy Carter, modified for special missions. Three boats, about $3 billion each, with Jimmy Carter around $3.5 billion. They were the most expensive attack submarines ever built.

*(Beat.)*

So was the Seawolf waste?

Here's where the thread gets interesting. The money spent designing the Seawolf was meant to be spread across twenty-nine submarines. Spread across three, each boat carries a staggering share of that cost. But nobody decided to build three expensive submarines. Someone decided to build twenty-nine, and then the world changed and someone else decided to stop.

What did stopping cost? A 2026 analysis in 19FortyFive points out that Seawolf construction beyond the first boat stopped in January 1992, but the first phase of design for its replacement, the Virginia class, wasn't approved until August 1994. The submarine yards and their suppliers lost momentum in that gap, and some of it never came back.

The Virginia class was meant to be the affordable answer: most of the Seawolf's capability at a much lower cost. And it is the Navy's workhorse today. But follow the money forward. From fiscal year 2011 through 2024, the Navy ordered two Virginias a year. The shipyards have never actually delivered two a year. Since 2022 they've delivered about 1.1 to 1.3. In fiscal 2025, the Navy dropped its order to one. A Virginia with the extra missile module now costs about $5 billion. Per-boat cost has climbed from about $2.7 billion in 2017. In May 2026, the Chief of Naval Operations told appropriators the yards should reach two a year by about 2032.

*(Beat.)*

So maybe the Seawolf's waste wasn't the three boats. Maybe it was the decade after them, when the country decided the submarine business could be turned down like a thermostat and turned back up later. That isn't how a shipyard works. Welders retire. Suppliers close. The know-how goes home and doesn't come back when the budget does.

That's the first time this episode finds the real thing it's looking for. The waste isn't only dollars. It's years.

## PART SIX — THE ONE THEY GOT RIGHT

*(Black and white: USS New Jersey firing broadsides off Vietnam. Color: Missouri firing in the Gulf, 1991.)*

Before going further, the episode owes the audience an honest exception.

The author's notes originally put the Iowa-class battleships in the waste ledger. The research doesn't support that, so they come out, and they come back in as the counterexample.

In 1981, the Reagan administration's Navy Secretary, John Lehman, wanted a 600-ship navy to face a growing Soviet fleet. The Soviets had just put the Kirov, a huge nuclear-powered missile cruiser, to sea. Lehman's answer was not to design something new. The Navy already owned four World War II battleships, the Iowa class, mothballed but intact. Wake them up.

Lehman told the Senate it was the quickest and most cost-effective way to get more naval muscle to sea in the mid-1980s. Each ship got Tomahawk and Harpoon missiles, modern radar and electronics, and kept its nine 16-inch guns. One recent account puts the whole program at about $1.7 billion, roughly $5 billion in today's money, which is less per battleship than a new frigate costs now. Critics like Norman Polmar put the 1980s cost higher, at more than $2 billion. Either way, the Navy's argument through every hearing was that each reactivation cost about as much as an Oliver Hazard Perry frigate, the cheapest escort in the fleet.

And there's the lineage. Bringing New Jersey back for Vietnam in 1968 cost $21.5 million. On the gunline she fired 5,688 sixteen-inch shells in about 120 days. Each ship had cost about $100 million to build in the 1940s.

In Desert Storm in 1991, Missouri and Wisconsin fired Tomahawks and 16-inch guns at Iraqi targets. Then the Cold War ended, and the battleships went back into reserve and eventually became museums. That's what you do with something you no longer need.

*(Beat.)*

The objections were real: thousands of sailors per ship when the Navy was short of petty officers, old steam plants nobody was trained on anymore, a ship with almost no defense against aircraft or submarines that needed escorts. Polmar argued in 2005 that bringing them back again would buy virtually no useful capability, and he was probably right about 2005.

But in 1981, the Iowas were exactly what this episode keeps saying the Navy forgot how to do: use what you already own, modify it modestly, get it to sea fast and cheap, and retire it when the job is done.

The Navy has done the cheap, fast, proven thing before. It didn't need to learn how. It needed to remember.

## PART SEVEN — THE LEDGER

*(On screen: a plain table. The banner INFERENCE · AUTHOR'S ARITHMETIC stays up for the entire section.)*

Now the arithmetic. The rules for this part are strict: every input on the screen, every input sourced, and the viewer can redo the sum.

The author's earlier notes added up a figure of about $76 billion. On re-sourcing, part of that rested on a Ford "over budget" number that doesn't hold up cleanly. So this is a more conservative count.

Four lines. The Zumwalt class: about twenty-four and a half billion dollars, research and development included. The hypersonic refit on top of it: at least two billion more. The Ford premium, the gap between Fords one through four and four more Nimitz carriers at today's prices: about twenty-two and a half billion. And the Littoral Combat Ships retired early: about seven billion. Together, roughly fifty-six billion dollars.

| Item | What's counted | Figure |
|---|---|---|
| Zumwalt class | Program cost for 3 ships, including R&D (GAO 2018) | ~$24.5B |
| Zumwalt hypersonic refit | Conversion of 3 ships (GAO July 2026), missiles excluded | at least ~$2B |
| Ford class premium | Fords 1–4 (~$56.0B) vs. "Nimitz 11–14" at the Bush price adjusted for inflation (~$33.4B), class R&D excluded | ~$22.5B |
| Littoral Combat Ship | Early decommissioning loss (Defense News, from CBO data) | ~$7B |
| **Total** | | **~$56B** |

At the FY2026 Flight III Burke price of about $2.7 billion each, that's around twenty destroyers. At about $5 billion per Virginia, it's around eleven submarines.

*(Ghost hulls fill a harbor: twenty destroyers. They dissolve. Eleven submarines. They dissolve.)*

Now the honest caveats, because the series doesn't let its own numbers off the hook:

- The Ford premium uses ordinary consumer inflation, while shipbuilding costs rose faster, and it ignores the Ford's added capability. That makes it somewhat harsh to the Navy. It also leaves out the class research and development, which makes it generous.
- The ledger leaves out the Constellation's sunk costs, which aren't cleanly public; the Seawolf, for the reasons above; and the cost of the years. That makes it generous to the Navy.
- And the deepest caveat: money not spent on these ships wouldn't automatically have become destroyers and submarines. The shipyards are the bottleneck. Extra money on its own wouldn't have built seventeen more Burkes at two a year.

That last point is the one that matters most. So follow it.

## PART EIGHT — THE SHIPYARDS

*(Bath, Maine. Pascagoula. Newport News. Groton. Then: Chinese yards, wide shots, cranes to the horizon.)*

The number that haunts every naval planner is from a leaked 2023 slide by the Office of Naval Intelligence. It put China's shipbuilding capacity at about 23.2 million tons against less than 100,000 tons for the United States. That's more than two hundred times greater.

The comparison isn't completely fair. China's figure folds in its huge commercial industry, which builds a large share of the world's merchant ships, and America's figure counts mostly naval yards. But that unfairness is the point. In a long war, a country's commercial yards become its naval yards. China has them. The United States mostly gave its commercial yards away decades ago.

The same slide showed battle forces. In 2020 the Chinese navy had 355 ships and the U.S. Navy 296. By 2035 it projected 475 for China against 305 to 317 for the United States. The Pentagon's 2024 China report put the PLA Navy at more than 370 ships, the world's largest navy by hull count. The U.S. Navy stood near 296 battle force ships in early 2025 and planned a deployable force of 287 for fiscal 2026. A report published ahead of the Euronaval show in October 2026 found that China built more than twice the naval tonnage of the United States in 2024 and 2025.

*(Beat.)*

The Navy's own counter is quality. A Virginia is a better submarine than anything China has. American carrier aviation, logistics, and allies have no real peer. All true, today.

But quality has to show up in numbers eventually, and the American numbers are thin. Every Arleigh Burke still being built is a design whose first ship joined the fleet in 1991. It's a proven ship, a legion ship. And the FY2027 budget request asked for only one, about $3.3 billion, when the line had been producing two a year. The Chief of Naval Operations said he'd try to find money for a second.

So the most successful surface warship program in the Navy's modern history is being slowed down for lack of money, in the same decade the Navy spent billions on frigates it then cancelled.

*(Beat.)*

There's an older piece of this story. In 1993, at a Pentagon dinner later nicknamed "the Last Supper," defense executives were told to expect fewer programs and to consolidate. Over the following years dozens of major contractors merged down to a handful. The government got efficiency. It also lost leverage. When a program fails and only one company can build that kind of ship, who does the Navy threaten to go to instead?

*(On screen: recheck the "51 to 5" consolidation figure before recording.)*

## PART NINE — WHO PAYS

*(A congressional hearing room. Empty chairs. A GAO report, stacked, unread.)*

The episode wants to ask a question, and it wants to be careful asking it.

When a program goes this wrong, who is responsible?

Go looking and you mostly find reports. GAO reports, year after year, sometimes warning years in advance. GAO's 2008 report on the Zumwalt was titled, in so many words, "Cost to Deliver Zumwalt-Class Destroyers Likely to Exceed Budget." GAO warned about the Ford's immature technologies before the ship was built. The warnings were written down, printed, filed and largely ignored.

There's a real argument that this isn't anyone's crime. Big programs fail through thousands of individually defensible decisions made by people who rotate out of their jobs every two or three years. The admiral who started the Zumwalt wasn't the admiral who cancelled it. The secretary who chose the Constellation wasn't the one who killed it. Nobody owns the whole arc, so nobody owns the failure.

*(Beat.)*

And that, maybe, is the actual answer. Not corruption, not conspiracy. A system that's designed so the people who make the decisions are gone before the consequences arrive.

The series has said it before in other words: institutional memory goes when the people go. In World War II, America knew how to build the same ship again and again, quickly, in enormous numbers. Liberty ships, Fletcher-class destroyers. Somewhere between then and now, it forgot.

## PART TEN — A ROMAN DETOUR

*(Banner: INTERPRETATION · ONE READING. Ancient Mediterranean. A cargo ship riding low in the water, then sinking on purpose.)*

The research turned up an old story that the episode didn't go looking for.

In 212 BC, during Rome's war against Hannibal, the Roman state was desperate to keep its armies in Spain supplied. It contracted with private companies, the publicani, to ship supplies, and it agreed to cover their losses if ships went down in storms.

According to the historian Livy, some of the contractors saw an opportunity. One of them, Marcus Postumius of Pyrgi, loaded old, worthless ships with small amounts of cheap cargo, sank them at sea, rescued the crews, and then claimed compensation from the state for full cargoes. When the tribunes finally brought him to trial, his allies among the contractors tried to break up the assembly with a riot.

*(Beat.)*

It's a vivid story, and it's tempting to make too much of it. Rome didn't fall because of insurance fraud in 212 BC. In fact it won that war. Historians argue about why Rome fell, and no serious historian thinks there's a single cause. Edward Gibbon blamed a loss of civic virtue. Others point to plague, to money, to the frontier, to politics.

But the shape of the Postumius story is familiar. A state in a hurry. Contractors who understood the state's rules better than the state did. Losses paid for whether or not the ships existed.

*(On screen: INTERPRETATION. This parallel came from the research rather than being reached for. It's offered as a resemblance, not as proof.)*

The difference, the episode has to admit, is that Postumius was a crook. The Zumwalt wasn't fraud. The Ford wasn't fraud. They were ambition and optimism and a system with nobody holding the whole thread. That may be harder to fix than crookedness, because there's nobody to put on trial.

## PART ELEVEN — THE COUNTER-THREAD

*(Not triumphant, just steady. A B-21 rolling out. A Virginia launching. A Burke under construction.)*

There's a version of this episode that ends in despair. The series refuses it, not to make anyone feel better, but because despair isn't accurate either.

Look at the last couple of years. The Constellation was cancelled fast once the numbers stopped making sense. That's a lesson the Navy failed to learn for decades with other programs. Its replacement starts from a hull already in production. The Defense Secretary complained publicly in November 2025 about an "absence of urgency." Congress has put billions into the submarine industrial base. The Virginia class is ramping, slowly. The Zumwalts, for all their history, are about to carry a weapon nobody else at sea has. And in Episode 1, a bomber built on a fixed-price deal is flying on something close to schedule, with its builder having absorbed billions in losses rather than passing them on.

The golden era and the wasted years aren't opposites. They've been happening at the same time.

*(Beat.)*

The real question isn't whether America wasted money. It did, and the GAO reports say so in plain language. The question is whether the correction is arriving in time, and whether the years can be bought back the way dollars can.

Dollars can be re-appropriated. A shipyard workforce takes a generation. A welder who retired in 2009 isn't coming back. A supplier that closed in 1995 doesn't reopen because a budget line changed. China's yards are busy every day.

*(Beat.)*

The author's notes had a sentence for this: history may show that this was part of how the American century ended. Maybe. Or history may show that this was the decade when America noticed and started remembering what it once knew how to do. Use what you have, build it again and again, don't put twenty-three new things on one ship, and when something doesn't work, stop sooner.

The episode doesn't know which. That's the honest ending.
