## COLD OPEN

*(Six old children's books on a shelf. No music.)*

There is a set of children's books in a house in Baton Rouge, Louisiana. Six volumes. The set is called My Book House, and it is old now, older than almost anyone who might pick it up.

A man used to read from these books to his daughter. She was four. She remembers that he was happy, that he laughed a great deal, and that he loved the Army. Those are the memories of a four-year-old, and they are nearly all she has, because one day he left for a place called Korea and the reading stopped.

It did not stop for long. Her mother picked up the books and kept going, the same six volumes, in the same house, in a different voice.

*(Beat.)*

The man was Lieutenant Colonel Don Carlos Faith Junior. He was thirty-two years old.

---

## PART ONE — THE FIRST NIGHT

On the night of November twenty-seventh, 1950, he was standing on the east shore of a frozen lake in the mountains of North Korea. The American maps called it Chosin. The temperature was around zero Fahrenheit and falling. His battalion had arrived first, ahead of everyone else, and so it held the position farthest north, the most exposed ground on that side of the water. The plan was to attack at dawn.

Nobody on that shore knew that a Chinese division had already moved in around them. And here is a strange detail, the kind this series likes to stop for. The Chinese did not know who they were surrounding either. They believed they were about to attack United States Marines. The Marines had left the day before. What sat in their place was an Army battalion that had been there for about one day.

The attack came late that night. Telephone lines were cut. One of Faith's company commanders was killed, and the man who took over that company was not an infantry officer at all. He was a Marine captain named Edward Stamford, who had been sent along to talk to airplanes.

By morning the battalion had taken about a hundred casualties and was still holding. And Don Faith had four more days to live.

---

## PART TWO — THE HELICOPTER

The next afternoon, a helicopter came in over the ice.

In 1950 that was still an unusual thing to see. Out of it stepped Major General Edward Almond, who commanded every American on that side of Korea, and with him a young aide, a first lieutenant named Alexander Haig. Remember that name. The thread may come back to it some day.

Faith tried to tell the general what had happened in the night. He said his battalion had been hit by two Chinese divisions. Almond did not accept it. The enemy in those hills, he said, was nothing more than the remains of units running north. The attack would go on. They were going all the way to the Yalu River.

Then the general did something that every account of this battle stops to describe. He reached into his pocket and took out three Silver Stars. One was for Faith. He told Faith to choose two other men for the others. Faith picked a lieutenant who had been wounded, and a mess sergeant who happened to be walking past. The medals were pinned on. The helicopter lifted off and went back the way it came.

Faith watched it go. Then he tore the Silver Star from his own jacket and threw it into the snow. An officer near him heard what he said: "What a damned travesty."

*(Beat.)*

Stop on that for a moment, because it is easy to enjoy this scene too quickly. A tired officer, a foolish general, a gesture. But consider who is making the gesture.

Don Faith was a general's son. He grew up on Army posts. What he wanted most was to go to West Point, and in 1937 West Point turned him down. The reason was his teeth. A few years later the draft board called him in, examined him, and turned him down again, for the same teeth. The United States Army looked at this man twice and said no.

He got in anyway, the long way around, through Officer Candidate School, and was commissioned in February of 1942.

So this is a man who had to fight to be allowed to wear the uniform at all. A decoration for courage, pinned on by a general, in front of his men. For most of his life that was the thing he wanted. And he ripped it off and threw it away, because it had been handed to him in place of the truth.

There is a footnote, and this series collects footnotes. After he was dead, the Army awarded Don Faith a Silver Star for that same day, November twenty-eighth. It is in his record now. The medal he threw into the snow followed him anyway.

Nobody knows where the first one is. It is presumably still there.

---

## PART THREE — THE ENEMY IN THE HILLS

All through that day, the men on the east shore could see them. Columns of Chinese soldiers on the ridgelines, in plain view, walking south. Not running north, as the general had said. Walking south, past the Americans, toward the only road out.

At midnight they came again. This time the fighting was inside the perimeter, hand to hand in places. Prisoners were taken, and the prisoners gave the names of their units: the 80th Division, and parts of the 81st. Two divisions. The number Faith had given the general that afternoon.

It is worth asking who those soldiers were, because the general's answer was contempt, and contempt is not information.

They belonged to one of the best formations China had. Its ranks were full of veterans of the civil war, the war that had just ended with their side winning. A few months earlier this same army had been on the coast, preparing to invade Taiwan. Then Korea happened, and they were sent north instead, into these mountains, in quilted cotton uniforms made for a milder climate. Many had little to cover their hands. Some prisoners were found wearing canvas shoes. The cold that was breaking American weapons was killing them where they lay. By the end of this battle, China's own count would show more men lost at this reservoir to frostbite than to American fire. And still they came down the hills every night.

There is a small thing about the name of the place. The Koreans call this lake Changjin. Chosin is the Japanese reading of the same name, and it was the Japanese who had made the maps. So here were Americans, fighting Chinese, in Korea, reading Japanese maps. The whole first half of the century is folded into that one word.

And one man on that shore had more of that century in him than most.

Don Faith had been a child in China. His father was an Army officer and China was one of his postings, so a part of Faith's boyhood was spent there. Then, in 1948, the Army sent him back. He was a lieutenant colonel by then, assigned to the American military mission. It did not last. The side America was advising was losing the civil war, and the mission was withdrawn. He went to Japan and was given a battalion.

Two years later, the army that had won that civil war was coming down a hill at him in the dark.

What did he make of that? There is no record. He left no diary from those five days, and the men who might have heard him talk about it mostly did not come home. But set the two facts next to each other. A general whose career had taken him to the Philippines, to Italy and to Tokyo, but never, so far as his record shows, to China, looked at these soldiers and saw a rabble. A battalion commander who had lived among them as a boy, and watched their army win as a man, looked at the same hills and counted two divisions.

One of them was right.

*(Beat.)*

At two in the morning, the order came to pull back.

---

## PART FOUR — COMMAND

The order came from Colonel Allan MacLean, who commanded the whole force on the east shore. There were two groups of Americans on that road, a few miles apart, and neither could survive alone. Faith's battalion was to leave its position and join the other group to the south.

They moved before dawn, with the wounded loaded on the vehicles. To reach the southern position they had to cross a frozen arm of the lake, under fire. Faith stood in the open and directed the vehicles across. When the last of the wounded were over, he crossed.

And then came one of the saddest minutes of the whole battle. As the column neared the southern position, Colonel MacLean saw soldiers coming up the road from the south. He had been waiting for days for one of his own battalions, which was late. He believed this was that battalion, and he saw that his men were firing at it. He ran out onto the ice, alone, waving, shouting at them to stop.

They were not his battalion. They were Chinese. He was hit several times. Faith's men watched as soldiers came out and dragged him away. He died four days later, somewhere on the road to a prison camp.

*(Beat.)*

So on the morning of November twenty-ninth, the senior officer left alive on the east shore of the reservoir was a lieutenant colonel, thirty-two years old, who had been turned down by West Point. He now commanded two battalions of infantry, the artillery, and several hundred wounded. Years afterward, a historian would give this force a name. He called it Task Force Faith. Nobody used that name in 1950. Don Faith never knew that anything had been named for him.

Where does a man learn to do this? Go back seven years.

July, 1943. The island of Sicily. The 82nd Airborne Division is about to carry out the first large parachute assault in the history of the United States Army, and its commander is a general named Matthew Ridgway. Beside Ridgway, carrying his maps, is his aide, a captain of twenty-four. Don Faith.

Sicily goes wrong almost at once. On the first night the wind scatters the paratroopers across the island, miles from where they are supposed to be. On the second night it is worse. The transport planes bringing in reinforcements fly over the Allied fleet, and the fleet opens fire on them. American guns shoot down twenty-three American aircraft, full of American paratroopers.

Ridgway had seen this coming. Before the invasion he had gone from headquarters to headquarters, warning that exactly this could happen. He was right, and it happened anyway. There is a photograph taken two weeks later, near a town called Ribera. Ridgway stands looking out over the battlefield, and at his side is Captain Faith.

What does an aide learn from a week like that? It cannot be known. But he had a close view of a commander whose plan fell apart through no fault of his own, who had been ignored by the people above him, and who simply went on commanding. The Army's own historians, describing Faith in battle years later, said he was very nearly a copy of Ridgway: intense, fearless, aggressive, and hard on anyone who was too careful.

Now look at what that copy of Ridgway was holding on the last two days of November.

He had one radio that could reach the outside world, and it belonged to Captain Stamford, the Marine. By day, Stamford brought in aircraft that kept the hills clear. By night there were no aircraft. Supplies were dropped by parachute, and some of them drifted to the enemy. A few miles south there was a company of American tanks. Twice the tanks tried to break through to him, and twice they were stopped.

On November thirtieth his own division commander, General Barr, flew in by helicopter and told him the truth. Everyone was pulling back. No help was coming. He would have to fight his own way out, carrying his wounded. That same afternoon, the tanks to the south were ordered to withdraw. The historian who studied this battle most closely wrote that the order signed the death warrant of the task force.

And at headquarters, that same week, General Almond said that if Faith did not attack as ordered, he should be relieved of command. General Barr refused. He said Faith was an excellent officer and that he had great confidence in him.

That night the Chinese attacked again, and the line very nearly broke. By morning there were close to six hundred wounded.

One more thread, before the trucks. Less than a month later, the American commander in Korea was killed in a road accident, and a new general was sent out to take over a beaten army. He rebuilt it, and most historians say he turned the war around. His name was Matthew Ridgway. He arrived about three weeks after his old aide died.

## PART FIVE — THE TRUCKS

December first. Faith decided at dawn. They would go that day, all of them, south down the road to the Marines at a place called Hagaru-ri.

There were about thirty trucks. Into them went the wounded, close to six hundred men, stacked on top of one another because there was no other way to fit them. Everyone who could walk would walk, and fight alongside. Many of the men were so exhausted that they did not know a breakout was planned until they saw the trucks lining up.

There was another way out, and it matters that he did not take it. The lake was frozen. Men on foot could walk across the ice, straight to the Marines, away from the road and the hills above it. But the ice would not hold a loaded truck. To go by the ice was to leave the wounded. Faith took the road.

The column moved at about one in the afternoon, with Marine aircraft overhead. Within minutes one of those aircraft dropped its napalm short. It fell on the front of the column, on the Americans. The accounts do not agree on the number, but men were killed and others burned, in front of everyone, by their own side. The same thing had happened in Sicily, seven years before, when he was Ridgway's aide.

He got them moving again. Then the bridge. The Chinese had blown it, and the trucks had to be dragged across the gap one at a time, by a winch, while rifle and machine-gun fire came down from the high ground into the truck beds. It took about two hours. Drivers were shot at the wheel. Other men climbed in and took their places, knowing what had happened to the last one.

Leave the road for a moment.

In November of 1942, nine months after he was commissioned, Don Faith married a woman named Barbara. Then he went to war, and the record says he was in every combat jump his division made, from Sicily to Germany. After the war there was a daughter, and they named her Barbara too, though she has always been called Bobbie. Then China. Then Japan. Then this.

So the man walking beside those trucks had a wife who had already waited through one war, and a four-year-old who liked to be read to. He knew what a wounded man in a truck was. It was somebody's Don Faith.

By late afternoon the column reached a hill the maps called 1221. The road bent sharply around its foot, the Chinese held the top, and at the bend they had built a roadblock. From there, as the light went, the men could see something to the south. The lights of Hagaru-ri. The Marines were that close.

Faith gathered everyone who could still carry a weapon, including wounded men who climbed down from the trucks, and sent them up the hill.

---

## PART SIX — THE HILL

And here the story has to say something that the citation for his medal does not.

Two soldiers refused to go. They were South Koreans, from the several hundred Korean troops attached to the American unit, many of them, as far as is known, conscripted only weeks before.

In that minute, on that road, a refusal was not a private act. Six hundred wounded men lay in the trucks behind them. The light was going, and when it went the aircraft would go with it. If the hill was not taken, none of those men would see morning. And a refusal that is allowed to stand, in front of soldiers who have not slept in five days, becomes ten refusals, and then there is no attack at all.

Faith drew his pistol and shot them both. Then he led the attack himself.

*(Beat.)*

There is no gentle way to tell that, and this series will not look for one. He did not stop to weigh how it would read in a report, or what it might do to his name, or to his career. He had no reason to think he would have a career. He weighed two men against six hundred, and he was responsible for the six hundred. It was a hard decision, made in seconds, by the one man on that road whose job it was to make it. He made it, and he did not send anyone up that hill ahead of him.

Now put that next to the general in the helicopter. Almond made hard decisions that week too. The difference is where each man was standing. Almond made his and flew away from them. Faith made his on foot, at the bottom of the hill, and he would be dead within hours. He asked nothing of anyone that he was not doing himself.

Leadership is not clean. It is not supposed to be. It is supposed to be responsible.

---

## PART SEVEN — THE ROADBLOCK

The hill was partly taken. The roadblock was still there.

Faith put himself at the front of another group of men and led them straight at it, firing his pistol and throwing grenades. He got to within about thirty yards. Then a Chinese grenade went off beside him, and the fragments went into his chest.

He was still giving orders when the roadblock was overrun.

What happened next is told in more than one way, which is itself a fact about that night. In one account his men lifted him into the cab of a truck, where it was warm, and he bled to death there. In another, the truck was hit by gunfire and he was struck again. In a third, they propped him on the hood of his jeep and he died in the cold. One version names the driver, a private first class named Barney, and says that his truck was the only one to get through the last roadblock, and that in the end he had to leave it, with Faith's body still inside.

The column went a little farther. There was another blown bridge. It was fully dark now, and the aircraft had gone home. The Chinese came down to the road. They fired into the truck beds, where the wounded lay stacked and could not move, and they threw grenades in among them. The trucks burned.

*(Beat.)*

That has to be said as plainly as the two men at the foot of the hill. War is ugly, and it was ugly that night in both directions.

The men who could still move went out onto the ice.

The records give two dates for his death, December first and December second. He was thirty-two. He was the only battalion commander in the entire Korean War to receive the Medal of Honor. By the time the sun came up, the force that would one day carry his name no longer existed.

---

## PART EIGHT — THE ICE

All that night, men came in across the ice toward the Marine lines, alone and in small groups, many of them wounded, most of them frostbitten. By dawn there were more than six hundred.

A Marine officer named Olin Beall went out to look for more. He was fifty-two years old and had been a Marine for thirty years. He ran a motor transport battalion; rescuing soldiers was not his job. He drove out onto the frozen lake with a medic and one private, in a jeep pulling a sled, and he kept going back. He brought in over three hundred men.

About three thousand Americans and Koreans had gone up the east side of the reservoir. Something over a thousand reached the Marines. Of those, three hundred and eighty-five could still fight. They were formed into a battalion, and they fought their way to the sea alongside the Marines. More than a thousand of the others were dead, or would die as prisoners.

And for a long time, that was told as a story of failure. Reports at the time said the unit had been routed. The men who survived it carried that for decades.

Then the record caught up. A historian went through it all in the 1980s. The two Chinese divisions that fell on Task Force Faith were so badly hurt that they were not seen in battle again until the following April. For five days they had been kept off the road at Hagaru-ri, the road the Marines needed in order to get out. In the year 2000, fifty years late, the task force was awarded a Presidential Unit Citation.

The last episode of this series was about people who built great things and got nothing. This is the same thread, pulled one step further. What is owed to the ones who were left on the ice?

---

## CLOSE — SIXTY-TWO YEARS

In Washington, on June twenty-first, 1951, General Omar Bradley handed the Medal of Honor to Barbara Faith. Her husband's status was changed, in time, to a phrase the Army uses: killed in action, body not recovered.

His father was still alive. Brigadier General Don Carlos Faith Senior died in 1963 and was buried at Arlington National Cemetery. Before he died he made an arrangement. On the back of his own gravestone, he had a second inscription cut: his son's name, his rank, the words Medal of Honor, and two years. 1918. 1950. There was no body to bury, so he gave his son the back of his own stone.

And the four-year-old grew up. She kept in touch, year after year, with the offices whose work is to find the missing. In the 1990s the Army named a headquarters building after her father, and at the ceremony she met men who had been in his battalion.

In September of 2004, a team of Americans was allowed into North Korea to dig near the reservoir. They opened a grave that held the remains of many men, mixed together. One hundred and one fragments of bone came out of it and were sent to a laboratory.

It took eight years. In August of 2012, the laboratory was sure. Of the one hundred and one fragments, nineteen were Don Faith. The match was made with DNA from his brother, and from his daughter.

That October a man from the laboratory came to a house in Baton Rouge and sat down with Bobbie Broyles and told her everything they knew.

On April seventeenth, 2013, a caisson carried him through Arlington. He was buried beside his parents, next to the stone that had carried his name on its back for half a century. A general folded the flag and handed it to his daughter. She was sixty-six years old.

*(Beat.)*

She was four when the reading stopped.

She still has the books.
