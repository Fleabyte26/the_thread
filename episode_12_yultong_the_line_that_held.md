## What it is

The story of a single night — 22 to 23 April 1951 — when roughly 900 Filipino soldiers of the 10th Battalion Combat Team held a collapsing stretch of the United Nations line against a Chinese assault many times their size, and of the captain who refused the order to withdraw because his men, living and dead, were still on the hill. It is the valor thread carried to Southeast Asia: a feat of arms the Philippines never forgot and the West never knew.

## The thread it pulls

Same note as Chosin and Edson's Ridge — an officer who would not leave his men behind, and paid the price that purchases the story. Where Faith threw a Silver Star into the snow, Conrado Yap died dragging bodies off a hill he had been ordered to abandon. The through-question of the human thread holds: a civilization that forgets men like this — and the West did forget them — tells you something about the civilization.

## Cold open (third person)

"On the night of 22 April 1951, the Chinese Spring Offensive hit a thin seam in the United Nations line held by about nine hundred Filipinos. By the time the order came to pull back, a forward company was already gone, overrun on a hill above a village called Yultong. One captain heard the withdrawal order, and did not move. His men were still up there — the ones still fighting, and the ones who would never fight again."

## Verified facts — stated as facts

- The Battle of Yultong was fought 22–23 April 1951, near Yultong (Yuldong), now part of Yeoncheon, South Korea, during the Chinese Spring Offensive of the Korean War.
- The Filipino unit was the 10th Battalion Combat Team (10th BCT) of the Philippine Expeditionary Forces to Korea (PEFTOK) — the first of five Philippine BCT contingents — attached to the U.S. 3rd Infantry Division and reduced by earlier fighting to roughly 900 men. Its commander was Col. Dionisio S. Ojeda.
- The attacking force was the People's Volunteer Army 34th Division (identified as the 44th Division in Chinese sources), 12th Army. The Chinese struck the Turkish Brigade with artillery around 20:00 on 22 April, then hit the 10th BCT in waves from about 23:00.
- The 10th BCT's companies fought in isolation, and the battalion's drivers, clerks, cooks, and chaplain were pressed into the fight.
- Company B, led by Lt. Jose M. Artiaga Jr., held Yultong Hill until it was overrun. Chinese forces penetrated as far as the reserve Company C position.
- Col. Ojeda ordered a withdrawal. Yap's company (described in sources as the Tank Company, later the Heavy Weapons Company) was the only unit that did not comply.
- Capt. Conrado D. Yap led that company in a counterattack to recover survivors and the dead. He was killed in the action; the company brought out a squad of survivors, and sources state he recovered the body of Lt. Artiaga and those of three enlisted men.
- At first light, Company C — with two M24 Chaffee light tanks from the Reconnaissance Company and allied artillery — counterattacked and retook some of the lost ground.
- At 09:00 on 23 April, Gen. James Van Fleet ordered a withdrawal to Line Kansas; at 12:15 the 10th BCT was ordered back to Anhung-ri. On 24 April it was attached to the British 29th Brigade Group and later took part in the attempted relief of the Gloucestershire Regiment at the Imjin River.

### Captain Conrado Dumlao Yap
- Born 22 January 1921, Candelaria, Zambales; killed in action 23 April 1951, aged 30. Philippine Military Academy class of 1943; U.S. Army Armor School, Fort Knox, 1949. Service number O-1914.
- Posthumously awarded the Philippine Medal of Valor — the country's highest military decoration — conferred by President Elpidio Quirino, and the U.S. Distinguished Service Cross. On 27 July 2018 he was awarded South Korea's First Class Taegeuk Order of Military Merit.
- Described in the sources as the most decorated Filipino serviceman of the Korean War. Commemorated by a patrol-craft class, a Philippine Military Academy mess hall, a corvette, and sites in Zambales and Tarlac.

### Lt. Jose M. Artiaga Jr.
- Infantry officer (service number O-1966) who led the forward company on Yultong Hill and was killed in action there.

### PEFTOK, in total
- Across 1950–1953, roughly 7,500 Filipino soldiers served in Korea through PEFTOK (five successive Battalion Combat Teams); about 116 were killed. The 10th BCT, which fought at Yultong, was the first contingent.

### Memory and commemoration
- A memorial to PETFOK stands at Yeoncheon-eup, Yeoncheon-gun, Gyeonggi Province, South Korea, commemorating the stand at Yuldong-ri and the "Fighting Filipinos."
- The battle is marked by an annual commemoration; the 67th anniversary was observed in 2018 (the same year South Korea conferred its First Class Taegeuk Order of Military Merit on Capt. Yap).
- The Philippine Embassy in Seoul has re-unveiled memorials to the Filipino veterans of the Korean War, framing them as the "forgotten" soldiers — a framing that is itself the episode's point.

## Disputed or open points — flag these, do not smooth them over

- **Enemy strength.** Filipino accounts cite figures from roughly 15,000 in the lead element up to 40,000; the primary reference flags both as needing better sourcing. State it as "a Chinese force many times their number," not a precise count, unless a firmer source is found.
- **Casualties.** The figures do not agree across sources. The 10th BCT's official report gives about 12 killed, 38 wounded, 6 missing; the Yeoncheon memorial page gives 15 killed and 14 missing; a Yeoncheon tourism source claims 125 dead. Present the official report as the primary number and name the others as differing accounts. Chinese losses are given as more than 500 killed and 2 captured.
- **Artiaga's decoration — a genuine source conflict.** One source states Lt. Artiaga was awarded the U.S. Distinguished Service Cross; another states the Philippine Distinguished Conduct Star. Do not assert one until resolved; say "decorated posthumously for his stand on the hill" and footnote the conflict.
- **How Yap died — two citations, two accounts.** The Medal of Valor citation says he was killed by enemy fire while assaulting an emplacement roughly 300 yards away; the U.S. Distinguished Service Cross citation says he was mortally wounded moving toward a stricken soldier on the hill slope. This is not a problem to hide — it is the episode's most honest beat: two nations wrote down his last minutes differently, and both agree he died going toward his men, not away.
- **Yap's company designation.** Sources call it both the Tank Company and the Heavy Weapons Company. Note the ambiguity rather than picking silently.

## The human core

The decoration list is not the story. The story is the decision at the order to withdraw. Every other unit pulled back, which was the correct military call. Yap did not, because the hill still held his men — the wounded who could be saved and the dead who could not be left. He died recovering bodies. That is the whole of it, and it needs no inflation.

## Ancient parallel (candidate, owned as commentary — develop only if it holds)

The duty to recover the fallen is one of the oldest in the Western martial tradition, and older still in the Greek world: the long, savage fight over the body of Patroclus in the Iliad, where men die to keep a comrade's corpse from the enemy, is the same act Yap performed on a Korean hill in 1951. If the parallel is used, it is used as the series uses them — because the theme surfaced it, not because it decorates the segment.

## Closing open question (end on the question)

Twelve men, by the official count, died on that line, and one of them went back up a hill he had been told to leave. South Korea pinned its highest military honor on him sixty-seven years later, in 2018. The West, which he fought for, largely never learned his name. So the human thread's question returns, pointed east this time: a civilization remembers what it chooses to remember — and the measure of one may be whether it still knows the names of the men who would not leave their dead behind. End on that.
