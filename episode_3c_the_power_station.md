## COLD OPEN

*(F-35s on a ramp. Heat shimmer off the tailpipes.)*

The most-produced Western fighter of its generation has a problem, and it isn't speed or stealth.

It's electricity.

Every sensor, every processor, every jammer on an F-35 draws power and makes heat. The jet cools its electronics partly with air bled off the engine, and it bleeds more than the designers planned for. So the engine runs hotter and wears out sooner. The Pentagon's own phrase is that the F135 is being operated outside its design specifications, and several of the F-35's promised upgrades are waiting until the 2030s for an engine that can feed them.

*(Beat.)*

Meanwhile, over Chengdu, a tailless Chinese jet the size of a medium bomber flies on three engines. Nobody outside China knows exactly why. One of the leading explanations is the same word.

Electricity.

The jet engine used to be the thing that pushed the airplane. Somewhere around the F-35 it became the thing that powers the airplane. This episode is about that change, and where it goes, all the way to engines that stop being turbines halfway through the flight.

## PART ONE — POWER, HEAT, AND THE THIRD STREAM

*(F-35s on the ramp. Heat shimmer.)*

Start where the cold open left off: an engine asked for more electricity and more cooling than it was designed to give, running hot and wearing out early.

There were two ways out.

### The adaptive engine that wasn't chosen

GE had built a working answer. The XA100, developed under the Air Force's Adaptive Engine Transition Program, started testing at Evendale in December 2020. It's an adaptive cycle engine with three air streams instead of two. Software shifts the engine between a high-bypass mode for efficient cruise and a low-bypass mode for thrust. The third stream gives the engine a built-in heat sink for the electronics. GE claimed 10 percent more thrust and 25 percent better fuel efficiency than current fighter engines.

In March 2023, the Pentagon chose a core upgrade to the existing F135 instead. The reasons were cost, integration risk, and schedule. The F135 installed base was huge, and a retrofit that dropped into existing jets beat a whole new engine that would need a new integration program.

### The Engine Core Upgrade

So the Engine Core Upgrade it is. It's a drop-in core for all three F-35 variants, in new production and in retrofit. The point is to restore full engine life and add electrical power and cooling margin. Pratt passed preliminary design review in July 2024. It got a contract worth up to $1.3 billion in September 2025. On September 15, 2026, it completed the Risk Reduction Design Review. Ground testing is planned for late 2027, flight testing around 2030, and production around 2031. Several Block 4 capabilities that depend on the new core have slid past 2031, and some internal planning points to around 2033.

*(Beat.)*

So the F-35, the most-produced Western fighter of its generation, is waiting on its engine to grow up before it can carry everything it was promised.

## PART TWO — THE NEXT CONTEST

Meanwhile, the adaptive idea moved to the next airplane. Boeing won the F-47 sixth-generation fighter contract on March 21, 2025. Its engine will come from the Next Generation Adaptive Propulsion program, NGAP. Two contenders: GE's XA102, descended from the XA100, and Pratt & Whitney's XA103, which uses F135 tooling and supply chains. Both finished Assembly Readiness Reviews in May 2026. Pratt finished on May 8, GE on May 11. In September 2026, GE said it was buying hardware and testing subcomponents before building the first XA102.

The F-47 is supposed to fly for the first time in 2028. The production NGAP engine is expected around 2030. The Air Force has said the first flight will probably use an interim engine. TWZ points out there's historical precedent for a new fighter flying on a stopgap engine. The F-14 flew on TF30s for years before the F110 arrived.

*(On screen, small: TF30 → F-14A, 1973. F110 → F-14B/D.)*

The thread keeps folding back on itself.

## PART THREE — THE HEAVIES GET BRITISH-GERMAN HEARTS

And the oldest bomber is getting the most commercial engine of all. In September 2021, the Air Force picked Rolls-Royce's F130 to re-engine all 76 B-52H bombers: 608 engines, assembled in Indianapolis, derived from the BR725 that flies on Gulfstream business jets. The BR700 family came out of a 1990s joint venture between Rolls-Royce and BMW. The program cleared its full Critical Design Review on May 4, 2026, which moved it into prototype modification and flight test. Test engines are expected in 2027. The re-engining is estimated at around $15 billion, and the Pentagon Inspector General puts the B-52's wider modernization at $48.6 billion. The re-engined jets will be called the B-52J. Once modified, the plan is for the B-52 to fly into the 2050s, about a hundred years after the first one flew.

*(Beat.)*

The bomber that the B-1, and its F101 engines, was supposed to replace will outlive the B-1. And it'll do it on an engine from a business jet, designed by a British company in partnership with a German one and built in Indiana. The locked room has a lot of doors now.

## PART FOUR — THE THREE-ENGINE MONSTER

*(The December 2024 Chengdu footage. The June 2026 PLA video.)*

On December 26, 2024, people in Chengdu filmed a very large, tailless, diamond-winged aircraft flying over the city. Analysts called it the J-36, from the serial number 36011 painted on it. It had three engines: two fed by intakes on the sides of the fuselage, and a third fed by a separate intake on top. No fighter had ever been built that way. At least three or four prototypes have been seen since. On June 28, 2026, the PLA put a few seconds of it in an official video. That was Beijing letting the world know it exists.

Estimates of its size vary. Analysts guess a maximum takeoff weight of fifty to sixty tonnes. One analysis gives a length of about 75 feet and a weapons bay about 25 feet long. It's closer to a medium bomber than to an F-22.

Why three engines?

There are three explanations going around, and they don't rule each other out.

**One: China's engines aren't strong enough yet.** This is the blunt one. Chinese engines still trail American and European engines in thrust, so adding a third may be the only way to push an airframe this big to supersonic speed. Quwa suggests the current engines might be WS-10C variants as an interim solution. Other analysts mention the WS-15 or the newer WS-19. None of this is confirmed.

**Two: endurance and supercruise.** More total thrust without afterburner means a heavy, long-range aircraft can cruise fast.

**Three: electricity.** This is the one the author keeps coming back to. Quwa, TWZ, and others have all made the case that the third engine is less about lifting the aircraft and more about powering it: a very large radar, high-output electronic warfare, the computing and datalinks to run drones at scale, and possibly directed-energy weapons, high-energy lasers or high-power microwaves. TWZ notes that there has been talk of a laser for the J-36 and is careful to add that fielding an airborne laser that can do real damage has historically been very hard, because of size, power, and cooling.

*(On screen: INFERENCE — AUTHOR'S HYPOTHESIS.)*

The author wants to push the third explanation one step further than the sources do. Suppose the dorsal engine isn't the same as the other two. Suppose it runs at a different bypass ratio, or is optimized for driving a generator and providing cooling airflow rather than thrust. Then the J-36 isn't really a three-engine fighter. It's a two-engine fighter carrying its own power plant. That would be China's answer to exactly the problem the F-35 is waiting until 2031 to fix, and that the XA102 and XA103 are designed to fix with a third air stream inside a single engine.

The American answer is one engine with three streams. The Chinese answer might be three engines doing different jobs. Nobody outside Chengdu knows. No public source has confirmed a different engine in the dorsal position. Hold this as a question, not a finding.

What isn't speculation is the shape of the problem. Every power-hungry thing a sixth-generation aircraft is supposed to do (sensors, jamming, autonomy, lasers) is ultimately limited by how much electricity and cooling the engines can supply. The engine stopped being only about thrust somewhere around the F-35. Now it's a power station that also flies.

## PART FIVE — PAST THE TURBINE

*(GE Niskayuna. Hermeus Quarterhorse. Wind tunnels.)*

And at the far end of the thread, the engine stops being just a turbine.

A turbine-based combined cycle engine, TBCC, pairs a turbojet or turbofan for takeoff and low speed with a ramjet, or a dual-mode ramjet and scramjet, for high speed. The hard part has always been the handover: getting the turbine to carry the vehicle fast enough for the ramjet to take over.

GE has spent about two decades building the pieces. One is a high-Mach turbine. Another is a rotating detonation combustor, which burns fuel in detonation waves circling an annular chamber rather than in steady combustion and promises better efficiency. At the Paris Air Show, GE revealed a DARPA-backed TBCC demonstrator that combines a rotating-detonation-equipped turbine with a dual-mode ramjet/scramjet, and said it was testing the transition at high supersonic speed. In January 2026, GE and Lockheed Martin announced direct-connect tests of a rotating detonation ramjet for hypersonic missiles, run at GE's research center in Niskayuna, New York. Both companies said the results beat expectations.

Then there's Hermeus, founded in 2018 in Atlanta. Its Chimera engine is a TBCC that hands over from turbojet to ramjet inside a single engine. The first Quarterhorse vehicles are built around a GE J85. The bigger Chimera II uses Pratt & Whitney's F100, the F-15 and F-16 engine, as its turbine section. In April 2026, Hermeus closed a Series C at a $1 billion post-money valuation.

*(Beat.)*

There's the repurposed F100. Not in a drone fighter, but in a hypersonic engine. The engine that stalled in the 1970s, got fixed by the Great Engine War, and pushed F-15s for fifty years is now being used as the low-speed first stage of something meant to go five times the speed of sound. Old cores don't die. They get repurposed.

## CLOSE — THE POWER STATION THAT FLIES

*(The F-35 ramp again. Then the J-36 footage. Then a Hermeus test cell, glowing.)*

The F-35 is waiting until the 2030s for a core that can feed its sensors. The F-47 will probably fly first on an interim engine while two adaptive engines fight it out. The B-52 is getting business-jet engines to fly for a hundred years. China put three engines in one jet, maybe because its engines are weak, maybe because it wants a power plant on board, maybe both. And past all of that, GE and Hermeus are building engines that hand off to ramjets at speeds where turbines can't go.

Every one of those stories is the same story: the engine stopped being a source of thrust and became a source of power. Whoever can make the most electricity and get rid of the most heat, in the smallest, lightest package, gets to carry the sensors, the jammers, the autonomy, and maybe the lasers.

The American bet is one engine with three air streams. The Chinese bet may be three engines with different jobs. Which bet is right, the episode doesn't know. That's the honest ending.
