## COLD OPEN

*(A regional airport at first light. One 737 at a remote stand. Nothing unusual about it. No music.)*

More than ten thousand 737s have been built. On any morning, at almost any airport in Asia, one of them is parked at a remote stand with its doors closed, and nobody looks at it twice.

Episode 3d said the arsenal jet should be one of those: an old 737-800 out of the desert, bought for about two million dollars and rebuilt for about ten. Episode 3f went into the hangar to see what that rebuild would take. It came out with a plan, pylons instead of a weapons bay, and with a problem it said twice. Six long-range anti-ship missiles cost about nineteen million dollars. The magazine costs twice what the airplane does.

*(Beat.)*

3f ended by asking who owns the drawings. That's a good question. The thread didn't follow it.

It kept turning over the nineteen million instead. And from that one number, four ideas came out, each one leaning on the one before.

What if the missiles were cheap? What if the airplane stayed dumb on purpose? What if the answer to the old wing was a new wing? And if this whole thing is a deterrent, where do the airplanes go on the day it matters?

That last one ends on a map. The map is where the idea gets strong, and where it gets into trouble, and then where it gets strong again.

---

## PART ONE — THE CHEAP MAGAZINE

When Episode 2 first drew the arsenal jet, the missiles it imagined were the expensive kind. That was reasonable at the time. It's less reasonable now, because in the last year the Pentagon started buying a different kind of missile.

The program is called FAMM, the Family of Affordable Mass Missiles. The Air Force's price target is about $218,000 a round. A JASSM, the standard American air-launched cruise missile, costs more than $1.3 million.

In July 2026 the Pentagon signed framework agreements with three companies. Anduril, with a missile called the Barracuda-500. CoAspire, with one called RAACM. And Zone 5 Technologies, with the AGM-188 Rusty Dagger. None of the three is one of the old prime contractors. The stated goal is as many as 8,000 missiles a year across the three once production ramps, with first deliveries in 2027.

There's a sister program for ground launch, announced in May, which the Pentagon said positions it to buy more than 10,000 low-cost cruise missiles in three years.

*(Beat.)*

One detail matters for a 737. FAMM comes in two forms. Palletized, which means pushed out the back of a cargo plane. And lugged, which means hung from a pylon like any other weapon. A 737 has no rear ramp. So the arsenal jet would carry the lugged kind, on exactly the pylons 3f spent its time on. The Rusty Dagger has already been flown on an F-16.

And one comparison belongs next to 3f. Two of these missiles came out of an earlier Air Force effort called ERAM, which went into production fourteen months after its first contract. 3f noted that putting one existing missile, LRASM, onto the P-8 had taken five years and wasn't finished.

Fourteen months to build a new missile. Five years to hang an old one. Something has changed in how this is done.

---

## PART TWO — WHAT CHEAP BUYS, AND WHAT IT COSTS

The author's instinct here is blunt. The old missiles were boutique items, built a few hundred at a time by companies that had no reason to hurry. The new ones go farther, weigh less and cost a fraction.

How much of that holds up?

Take the Barracuda-500. Anduril says it flies more than 500 nautical miles, carries a payload of more than 100 pounds, and cruises at up to 500 knots. That range is in the same class as the extended-range JASSM, and well past the ranges usually quoted for the original JASSM and for LRASM.

Weight is harder. Anduril hasn't published it. Reference sources say the number in the name is the weight class in pounds, so about 500. A JASSM is a 2,000-pound-class weapon. If that's right, the new missile matches the old one's reach at a quarter of the weight and a sixth of the price.

*(Beat.)*

So the instinct is mostly right. But it's worth saying how the trick is done, because it isn't magic.

A JASSM carries a warhead of about a thousand pounds inside a stealthy airframe. It was designed to get through good defenses alone and destroy something hardened when it arrived. The Barracuda carries a tenth of that. Take nine hundred pounds of warhead and the stealth shaping out of a missile, and a small jet engine will carry what's left a very long way.

That's a choice, and the old programs made the other one. And the old missiles still do things the cheap ones don't. LRASM can pick one particular ship out of a defended group by itself. A hundred-pound warhead won't sink that ship.

So the fair version is this. The cheap missile wins on reach, on weight and on price. It loses on punch, and on its odds of arriving alone. It's built to arrive in a crowd.

And the old companies have noticed. Lockheed Martin is offering a glide weapon it expects to sell for $150,000.

*(Beat.)*

Now put that on the airplane.

3f's plan was six LRASMs on six stations, for about nineteen million dollars. A P-8 wing pylon is rated at 3,000 pounds. If a cheap round weighs about 500, then by weight alone four of them fit on a station, with room left for the rack that holds them.

Six stations, four rounds each: twenty-four missiles. At the FAMM target price, about $5.2 million.

Four times the shots for about a quarter of the money. And for the first time in this series, the load costs less than the airplane carrying it.

That's the author's arithmetic. The thread hasn't found a rack that carries four of these on one pylon, and until someone builds one, twenty-four is a hope.

## PART THREE — AN AIRLINER THAT SHOOTS AT AIRPLANES

The author's idea goes one step further, and it's the strangest step. Put air-to-air missiles on the truck too. Very long-range ones.

That sounds absurd until you look at what the long-range programs have become.

The Air Force and Navy are bringing in the AIM-260, built in answer to China's long-range air-to-air missiles. It was supposed to be in service in 2022. In September 2026 the Pentagon signed a framework deal to speed up production, and declined to say how many or at what price. The Navy has fielded an air-launched version of a ship's missile, the AIM-174B, and in August it revealed another, the AIM-424, already in flight test.

And then there's the one this series has been getting slightly wrong. Since Episode 2, the thread has treated the Air Force Long Range Weapon, AFLRW, as a strike missile, the standoff magazine for the arsenal jet. The reporting describes it as an air-to-air missile first, with a target range of at least a thousand nautical miles, and a surface-attack version wanted as well.

A thousand nautical miles is not a dogfight. A missile like that isn't aimed by the airplane that carries it. Something far forward finds the target and guides the shot. The launching airplane only has to be in the air, pointed the right way, with missiles on it.

That's a description of a truck.

*(Beat.)*

The idea isn't new. Around 1960 the U.S. Navy designed an airplane called the F6D Missileer. It was slow and straight-winged, and it was meant to do one thing: orbit near the fleet with a load of very long-range missiles and let the missiles do the flying. It was cancelled before it was built. The objection was simple. Once its missiles were gone, it couldn't fight and couldn't run.

That objection hasn't gone anywhere. It applies to a 737 exactly.

And the cheap end of air-to-air barely exists yet. The Air Force has asked industry about a low-cost, high-speed air-to-air missile in two sizes: one the size of today's AMRAAM, built for range, and one half that size, so an airplane can carry twice as many. It's a request for information. It isn't a missile.

So the cruise-missile half of the cheap magazine is real and under contract. The air-to-air half is, for now, a good question.

## PART FOUR — THE DUMB TRUCK

The author added a rule, and it may be the most important sentence in the plan. The airplane is a truck. Always. Never a sensor platform.

It's tempting to break that rule. Two of the best sensor airplanes in the world are 737s: the P-8, which hunts submarines, and the E-7, which is a flying radar station. Once you're rebuilding a 737 anyway, why not give it a radar?

Because of what 3f found. The P-8 carries three times the wiring of an airliner, nearly a hundred antennas, and a crew of specialists. It costs $173 million. Every sensor you add makes the airplane more useful and more precious, until it's too valuable to send anywhere dangerous. Then it isn't a truck anymore.

So the truck gets pylons, wiring to the pylons, a way to release what's on them, and one smart thing: a datalink. A target comes in from somewhere else. The truck passes it to the missile and lets go.

*(Beat.)*

That keeps the ten-million-dollar airplane honest. It also keeps something else honest.

A dumb truck is worth exactly as much as the network that tells it where to shoot. If the drones forward are destroyed, if the satellites are blinded, if the link is jammed, then what's left is an airliner full of missiles with nothing to aim at.

Episode 11 said the real center of gravity in the Pacific is the kill chain: the layer that finds, decides and directs. This plan doesn't escape that. It leans its whole weight on it.

There's one more thing a truck has that a fighter doesn't. People who can drive it. The 737 is one of the two most common airliners on earth. The pilots already exist, in every country on the map this episode is about to draw. Whether an airline pilot would fly this mission is a different question, and a human one.

## PART FIVE — THE NEW WING

3f had two objections it couldn't answer. An old airliner's wing was never built to have 3,000-pound loads hung from it. And an old wing has a fatigue history, so every airplane would have to be checked one at a time.

The author's answer is the bluntest idea in the episode. Don't modify the wing. Replace it.

Build a new wing that bolts to the existing wing box. Same span. Fuel inside it, as before. And designed from the first drawing with three weapons stations under each side, plus a fourth at the tip.

*(Beat.)*

Half of that wing already exists. The P-8's wing is a strengthened 737 wing with two pylons a side, built on the same Renton line as the airline ones. The author's wing is that one, taken further.

And replacing the wings on an old fleet has been done. Starting in 2011, the Air Force put new Boeing-built wings on 173 A-10 attack jets, under a program worth about $1.1 billion. A second contract followed, worth up to $999 million for as many as 112 more sets. The A-10 was older than any 737-800 in the desert. It got a second life out of new wings.

Here is the part the plan has to face. Divide those contracts out, and an A-10 wing set runs somewhere between six and nine million dollars. A 737's wing is far larger than an A-10's. There's no public price for one, but it's hard to see it costing less.

So the new wing may cost more than the airplane it's bolted to. It may cost more than the whole ten-million-dollar rebuild that Episode 3d promised. The truck in this episode is probably a twenty or thirty million dollar airplane.

That's still a fraction of a new one. But 3d's headline doesn't survive, and the episode should say so plainly.

*(Beat.)*

What the money buys is real, though.

It answers both of 3f's objections at once. The hardpoints are designed in, with the wiring kept out of the fuel. And the part of an old airliner that wears out is new.

It also changes where the work happens. 3f pictured each airplane spending months in a hangar, one at a time, and worried that a hundred and thirty of them would take a decade. Wings can be built on a line, in a factory, in numbers, and then fitted. That's how the airline ones are made.

And it brushes against 3f's wall. A new wing is a new drawing. Whoever pays for it could own it. The joint where it meets the airplane would still be Boeing's.

Then the wingtip. The author wants a four-pack of air-to-air missiles out there.

Weight at a wingtip isn't crazy. It actually relieves the bending load on a wing in flight, which is why tip tanks work, and fighters have carried missiles there for decades. The danger is flutter. A 737 wing is long and flexible, and four full-size missiles and a launcher is the better part of a ton, hung at the very end of it, beside the ailerons.

But Part Three mentioned a missile half the size of an AMRAAM. Four of those weigh about what two full-size ones do. If that missile is ever built, the wingtip is where it belongs.

One idea in this episode keeps rescuing another. That's usually a sign of something. It isn't proof.

## PART SIX — EVERY RUNWAY

Now the map.

Episode 2 and Episode 3d both said this airplane is a deterrent. A fleet in being. Its job is to exist, and to be counted in someone else's plans.

The author's basing idea takes that seriously. In peacetime the trucks sit somewhere dry and far away. In a crisis they move. They go to every airfield that can land a 737 in Japan, the Philippines, South Korea, Australia, India, Singapore and Indonesia. And all across Taiwan.

To see why that would matter, start with how a Chinese planner is thought to see the problem today.

*(Beat.)*

In December 2024 the Stimson Center published a study of what Chinese missiles could do to American air bases. It said Chinese strategists regard the forward air bases, and the runways in particular, as the weak point of American airpower. Then it said, in its own words, "Unfortunately, Chinese strategists are correct."

Its model found that missile strikes could close the runways at American bases in Japan to fighters for about the first twelve days of a war, and to tankers for more than a month.

The Pentagon's 2024 report on China's military listed about 900 short-range ballistic missiles, 1,300 medium-range and 500 intermediate-range. An Air Force journal article that year made the comparison directly: the number of places the Air Force could disperse to in the Pacific is dwarfed by the number of Chinese missiles.

And a Hudson Institute study in January 2025 counted the other side's ledger. China has 134 air bases within a thousand nautical miles of the Taiwan Strait, with more than 3,000 aircraft shelters. American airpower in the region flies from a handful of bases, mostly in the open.

So today's arithmetic favors the missile. A few big bases, a known list, and more than enough missiles to cover it.

*(Beat.)*

Now change one thing. The airplane.

A fighter needs fuel trucks, weapons crews, shelters and spare parts. It can disperse, but only to places that have been prepared. A 737 needs what every 737 needs: a mile and a half of pavement, give or take, and jet fuel. Airports that can take one are not rare. They're what an airport is.

Japan alone has designated 24 civilian airports that its own forces can use, after adding more in April 2026. The Philippines has nine sites the United States can use under an existing agreement, several of them facing Taiwan or the South China Sea. Those are only the ones already on paper. The number of runways in those eight places that could physically take a 737 runs to several hundred. The thread hasn't counted them. Somebody should.

And the truck looks like an airliner. From orbit, on a ramp, among the others, a loaded one and an empty one are the same shape.

So the planner's list goes from a few dozen aim points to several hundred, and he can't tell which ones matter. He can try to hit them all, and spend his missiles on empty concrete. Or he can leave some, and accept that an unknown number of trucks will get airborne with twenty-four missiles each.

That's the author's plan, and it's the strongest form of the fleet in being this series has drawn.

*(Beat.)*

It has been tried before. On paper.

In September 1979, President Carter approved a way to protect America's new nuclear missile, the MX. Two hundred missiles would be shuttled at random among 4,600 shelters in the deserts of Nevada and Utah, twenty-three shelters for each missile. To be sure of destroying them, the Soviet Union would have had to hit every shelter. People called it the shell game.

It was never built. On October 2, 1981, President Reagan cancelled it, saying the scheme would be just as vulnerable as the silos it replaced.

But that wasn't the only thing that killed it. The people of Utah and Nevada didn't want to live among 4,600 targets. Ranchers said so. The Mormon church said so, publicly.

The shell game depended on the shells. And the shells turned out to have opinions.

## PART SEVEN — NOBODY ASKED THEM

The plan in Part Six lists seven countries and one island. It hasn't asked any of them.

So go down the list, honestly.

**Japan** is a treaty ally. But those 24 airports are designated for Japan's own Self-Defense Forces and coast guard, and the government has promised they stay civilian first. When Yamaguchi Ube Airport was added in 2025, a prefectural assembly member cited the Geneva Conventions and said that once the military uses a civilian airport, it becomes a target. He's from an opposition party. The legal point is a real one.

**The Philippines** is a treaty ally with nine agreed sites. Its government has been careful about what those sites are for, and Manila has its own citizens in Taiwan to think about, somewhere between 150,000 and 250,000 of them.

**South Korea and Australia** are treaty allies. Each would make its own decision in its own parliament, on its own timeline.

**India** is not an ally of the United States, and has never wanted to be. The logistics agreement the two signed in 2016 lets each use the other's facilities for fuel and supplies, case by case, by mutual consent. Indian officials have said from the start that it is not a basing agreement and nothing in it is automatic.

**Indonesia** has built its foreign policy since independence on a principle called bebas aktif, free and active: aligned with no one. In April 2026 Jakarta and Washington signed a defense cooperation partnership. That same month, a proposal to let American military aircraft cross Indonesian airspace on notification alone set off a national argument about sovereignty. Officials said no such agreement had been reached. And that argument was about flying over. This plan is about landing, loaded.

**Singapore** hosts American forces on rotation and has always stopped short of an alliance.

**And Taiwan.** The United States has had no bases there, and no defense treaty with it, since the end of the 1970s. American airplanes carrying cruise missiles, landing at airfields across the island in the middle of a crisis, could be the single act most likely to turn that crisis into a war. Taiwan's runways are also the closest of all to those 900 short-range missiles.

*(Beat.)*

So the map in Part Six is really two maps. One shows where a 737 can land. The other shows where a government would say yes, quickly, in the worst week of its decade. The second map is much smaller, and nobody knows its shape until the week arrives.

And there's a harder thought underneath. The plan says the trucks deploy in a crisis. That means the deterrent has to move at the most dangerous moment there is. From Beijing, hundreds of airliners fanning out to runways around China's coast, some unknown number of them armed, wouldn't look like a bluff. It could look like the hour before an attack. In 1914, each great power's mobilization was meant partly to warn the others off, and each one made the others move faster.

That's one reading. But a deterrent that only shows itself in a crisis is also a signal that the crisis has become something else.

There's an obvious repair. Make it routine. Fly the trucks through these airports in peacetime, in exercises, empty and announced, until it's as ordinary as a port call. That would take the alarm out of it. It would also require every one of those governments to say yes in public, in peacetime, over and over.

## PART EIGHT — WHAT THE PLANNER HAS TO ASSUME

The author's answer to Part Seven is short. What those governments would say matters less than what the planner has to assume.

A man planning a war can't build it on the hope that other countries will hold to their peacetime positions in a crisis years away. He plans against what's possible. If a 737 can land there, and the government hasn't ruled it out, the runway goes on his list.

And history gives him reasons to worry.

In 1940, Britain didn't ask Iceland. It landed troops. In 1941, Britain and the Soviet Union did the same to Iran. In 1943, Portugal, neutral, handed over airfields in the Azores under heavy pressure. In 1990, Saudi Arabia accepted American troops within days of Iraq taking Kuwait. In 2001, Pakistan opened its airspace after an American ultimatum. In a crisis, the room to say no can shrink very fast.

*(Beat.)*

The record runs the other way too, and the thread should say so. In 1973, almost every European ally refused to let the American airlift to Israel land or fly over. In 1986, France and Spain refused overflight for the raid on Libya, and the bombers flew around them. In 2003, Turkey's parliament voted down the passage of an American division into Iraq. Ireland kept its ports closed to Britain for the whole of the Second World War.

What separates the two lists looks like this. Countries said yes when the same enemy threatened them, or when a great power was willing to force the matter. They said no when it was someone else's war and saying yes would make them a target.

Nobody is going to force India or Indonesia. And a deterrent that had to coerce its own hosts would have no hosts.

But the planner has read all of this as well. He can't know which list a country will land on until the week arrives. So he has to carry the whole map.

*(Beat.)*

Now the author's number. Picture six to eight trucks at an airfield.

At twenty-four missiles each, that's between 144 and 192 cruise missiles, leaving from one ordinary airport.

3f imagined a fleet of about a hundred and thirty airplanes. At six to eight to a field, that fleet fills somewhere between sixteen and twenty-two airfields. Out of several hundred.

So nearly all of the runways on the planner's list would be empty. That's the design. The MX plan put one missile among twenty-three shelters. This is about the same ratio, with one difference: nobody had to pour the shelters. They're already there, and they're called airports.

And think about what covering them would mean. To be safe, he would have to strike hundreds of civilian airports, in seven countries, in the first hour, before the trucks are off the ground. That would make enemies of countries that might have stayed out. A war he wanted to keep small would have to begin by being made enormous.

That is the strongest thing this idea does. It doesn't have to win a fight. It raises the price of starting one.

*(Beat.)*

Three cautions, so the thread stays honest.

It only works if it's believable. A planner can discount something that has never once happened. A few close allies would have to host these airplanes in the open, in peacetime, before the rest of the map counts for anything.

He has other moves. He can work on the answer years ahead, with trade and diplomacy, in the capitals. He can go after the kill chain and leave the trucks blind. Or he can leave the airports alone and shoot down the missiles, which are slow and small.

And the cost lands on people who weren't asked. If he has to assume Indonesia says yes, then Indonesian airports are on a target list whether or not Indonesia ever agrees to anything. The same is true of India. An idea drawn up in one country puts another country's airports in a third country's war plan.

That is a real cost. The author's plan gets its strength from other people's silence, and it should say so.

## PART NINE — THE OTHER SIDE OF THE LEDGER

Now the rest of the case against.

**The truck is soft.** A 737 on an open ramp, loaded with missiles, has no shelter and no armor. In June 2025, Ukraine destroyed Russian bombers on their own airfields with small drones launched from trucks parked nearby. An airliner full of fuel and warheads at a civilian airport, with a public road beside the fence, is a much easier target than that.

**Civilian airports are full of civilians.** Putting a weapon on a civil ramp doesn't only make the airport a lawful target. It puts the terminal next to it.

**The idea is easy to copy.** China's airlines fly hundreds of 737s and Airbus A320s, and China builds an airliner of its own. Episode 11 called this the symmetry trap. Whatever is cheap and clever for one side is cheap and clever for the other.

**The missiles may not be enough.** Twenty-four hundred-pound warheads will wreck radars, fuel, parked aircraft and soft ships. They won't stop a fleet. A plan that swaps all of its heavy missiles for light ones has given up the hard targets.

**The wing broke the budget.** Part Five said it. This is no longer a ten-million-dollar airplane.

**The rack doesn't exist, the half-size missile doesn't exist, and the Missileer was cancelled for a reason.** Three of this episode's ideas rest on hardware nobody has built.

**And 3f's objections still stand.** Somebody has to own the engineering. The first article takes years. An airliner caught in the air doesn't survive.

## CLOSE — THE AIRPLANE NOBODY LOOKS AT

*(Back to the regional airport. Full daylight now. The 737 at the remote stand hasn't moved.)*

So go back to the airplane at the remote stand.

This episode started with a number, nineteen million dollars of missiles on a ten-million-dollar airplane, and tried to make the number smaller. It got there. A quarter of the cost, four times the shots, on a truck that knows nothing and carries a lot.

Then it tried to fix the wing, and made the airplane more expensive than it promised.

Then it drew a map, and found that the strongest part of the idea is also the part that belongs to other people. A runway is a physical thing. Anyone can measure it. Permission is not, and it can't be stockpiled in a desert.

*(Beat.)*

One reading is that this is the pattern of the whole arsenal-jet story. Every time the thread solves the hardware, the problem moves somewhere softer. From the airframe to the hangar. From the hangar to the drawings. From the drawings to the missiles. And now from the missiles to seven governments and an island, each with its own history of being someone else's battlefield.

Would a Chinese planner have to deal with it? If even a few of those governments said yes, he would. He'd have to plan against an airplane he can't tell from the ones around it, on runways he can't afford to hit and can't afford to ignore.

Would they say yes? The episode doesn't know. Neither does anyone in Washington, and neither do they, yet.

The author's point is that the planner doesn't know either, and he's the one who can't afford to guess wrong.

That's the honest ending. The cheapest part of this deterrent is the airplane. The most expensive part isn't for sale.
