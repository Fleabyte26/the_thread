Major General Merritt Austin "Red Mike" Edson (1897–1955) was a legendary United States Marine Corps commander, innovator in amphibious and guerrilla warfare, and a recipient of the Medal of Honor. He is remembered as one of the most inspirational combat leaders of the Pacific War, revered by the men who served under him for his coolness under fire, tactical brilliance, and refusal to ask his troops to do anything he would not do himself.

Origins of "Red Mike" and the Marine Raiders
Early Career: Born in Vermont, Edson joined the Marine Corps Reserve during World War I and earned his first Navy Cross during the "Banana Wars" in Nicaragua (1928–1929). There, he led patrols deep into jungle waterways against Sandinista rebels, growing a fiery red beard that earned him the lifelong moniker "Red Mike."

The Father of Marine Raiders: In late 1941 and early 1942, Edson spearheaded the formation of the 1st Marine Raider Battalion—elite, light-infantry units modeled after British commandos. He hand-picked his officers and men, instituting grueling physical conditioning, night-fighting drills, rubber-boat operations, and rigorous marksmanship training.

The Pacific Campaign & Battle of Edson’s Ridge
Edson led his Raiders in the initial assaults of the Pacific Theater, seizing Tulagi in August 1942 and conducting aggressive commando strikes on Savo Island and Tasimboko on Guadalcanal.

His defining hour occurred during the Battle of Edson’s Ridge (Bloody Ridge) on Guadalcanal on the night of September 13–14, 1942:

The Standoff: Edson’s battered force of roughly 830 men (his Raiders along with attached companies of the 1st Parachute Battalion) held a critical grassy ridge just south of Henderson Field, the airstrip essential to Allied control of the Solomon Islands. They faced over 2,500 elite Japanese infantry commanded by Major General Kiyotake Kawaguchi.

Leadership Under Fire: Waves of enemy charges breached the forward Marine lines, turning the battle into vicious, hand-to-hand combat in pitch darkness. Edson continuously exposed himself to hostile mortar, rifle, and machine-gun fire, walking the lines just yards behind the fighting positions, standing atop ammunition crates, and personally directing defensive fires.

Holding the Line: When lines wavered under massive human-wave assaults, Edson rallied his men directly, calmly correcting weapon positions and coordinating dangerously close defensive artillery barrages from the 11th Marines. His men repelled every assault, saving the airfield and turning the momentum of the Guadalcanal campaign.

For his conspicuous gallantry on the ridge, Edson was awarded the Medal of Honor.

Later War Service and Style of Leadership
Pacific Staff and Division Command: Edson later served as Chief of Staff of the 2nd Marine Division, playing a key planning role at Tarawa, and as Assistant Division Commander during the bitter fighting on Saipan and Tinian, earning a Silver Star and two Legions of Merit.

Quiet Intensity: Unlike flamboyant commanders, Edson was slight of build, soft-spoken, and methodical. War correspondent Richard Tregaskis (Guadalcanal Diary) described him as having a "calm, frosty demeanor and eyes of steel," calling him simply "the best soldier I ever knew." His men trusted him implicitly because he placed his command post where the fighting was thickest.

Post-War Legacy
After the war, Edson took on a fierce political battle in Washington during the late 1940s, testifying before Congress to protect the Marine Corps from being absorbed or downsized by the Army under the post-war military unification plans.

Following his retirement from active duty in 1947, he became the first Commissioner of the Vermont State Police and later the Executive Director of the National Rifle Association. Today, the Marine Corps marksmanship range at Camp Pendleton, California, is named Edson Range in his honor, and the Navy commissioned the Forrest Sherman-class destroyer USS Edson (DD-946) in his memory.

The contrast between the two is striking:

The Actor: John Wayne was classified 4-F and 2-A during World War II due to age (in his mid-30s), family status with four children, and ear/shoulder injuries from his football days. He spent the war years on Hollywood studio lots and tropical backlots in Burbank, filming classics like The Fighting Seabees and Back to Bataan, wearing a tailored uniform, hitting marks under hot lights, and projecting celluloid invincibility.

The Real Leader: Merritt Edson was out in the sweltering rot of the Solomon Islands, surviving on scrounged Japanese rice, battling recurring bouts of malaria, and standing on a literal crate under devastating mortar barrages while his men took shrapnel.

One of the great ironies of the era is that Wayne later felt deeply insecure about not serving in combat, which drove him to make some of the most overtly patriotic war films of his career. Meanwhile, men like Edson, Chesty Puller, or Audie Murphy never needed to act the part. Real courage doesn't require a booming voice, 6-foot-4 stature, or a script—it's forged in quiet resolve when everything around you is going to pieces.

Yet behind that slight frame and soft-spoken demeanor was iron resolve, extraordinary physical endurance, and absolute tactical clarity. His men quickly learned that genuine toughness doesn't come from physical size or loud talk—it comes from character, calm under pressure, and competence. It’s the ultimate proof that true presence and leadership are entirely internal.

Gunnery Sergeant John "Manila John" Basilone (1916–1945) was another towering figure in Marine Corps history and a legend of the Pacific War. He remains the only enlisted Marine in World War II to receive both the Medal of Honor and the Navy Cross.

The Stand at Henderson Field (Guadalcanal)
Like Merritt Edson, Basilone earned his Medal of Honor defending the perimeter around Henderson Field on Guadalcanal, serving under Lieutenant Colonel Lewis B. "Chesty" Puller in the 1st Battalion, 7th Marines:

The Night Attack (October 24–25, 1942): Roughly 3,000 soldiers from the Japanese Sendai Division hammered Basilone’s heavy machine-gun positions in torrential rain and darkness.

Fighting Through the Line: When one of his machine-gun sections was knocked out—leaving only two men functional—Basilone lugged an extra 90-pound water-cooled Browning machine gun into place, cleared jams, and personally fired into charging lines.

Securing Ammo Under Fire: When ammunition ran perilously low and rear supply lines were cut off by infiltrators, Basilone sprinted and crawled through hostile fire to bring back fresh belts of ammunition.

Three Days Without Rest: He and his men held their ground for three straight days and nights without sleep or relief, fighting hand-to-hand with pistols and machetes when weapons jammed or ran dry. His stand halted the Japanese regiment's breakthrough.
