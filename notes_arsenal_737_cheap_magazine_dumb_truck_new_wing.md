## Kris's three ideas

1. **The cheap magazine.** The weapons on the arsenal 737 should not be expensive. They should be mass-produced, low-cost cruise missiles, and even very long-range air-to-air missiles, drawing on all the current missile programs in this area.
2. **The dumb truck.** The 737 is a truck, always. It is never a sensor platform.
3. **The new wing.** The modification is an entire wing replacement, attached to the existing wing box: fuel, the same length, and 3 + 1 weapons stations per wing. Station 4 is the wingtip, holding a four-pack of air-to-air missiles.

Kris's framing of the missiles: the new ones have longer ranges and less weight than the old expensive ones, which were "built as boutique items by the dinosaur missile companies."

## 1. The cheap magazine — what exists (reported)

**FAMM, the Family of Affordable Mass Missiles (Air Force).**
- Cost target about $218,000 a round, against more than $1.3 million for a JASSM. (Quwa.)
- Three designs chosen in July 2026: Anduril Barracuda-500, CoAspire RAACM, Zone 5 AGM-188 Rusty Dagger. (Air & Space Forces Magazine; Military Times.)
- Up to about 8,000 missiles a year across the three once production ramps. Five-year production contracts authorized in the fiscal 2026 NDAA. (Air & Space Forces Magazine.)
- First deliveries in 2027. (Airforce Technology.)
- Two carriage styles: lugged (hung on a pylon) and palletized. (ExecutiveGov.) A 737 has no rear ramp, so the 737 uses the lugged versions. *(Inference.)*
- Rusty Dagger has been carried and tested on an F-16. (Air & Space Forces Magazine.)

**Related programs.**
- LCCM, Low-Cost Containerized Missiles (May 2026): Anduril, CoAspire, Leidos, Zone 5; ground-launched first. Pentagon positioned to buy more than 10,000 low-cost cruise missiles in three years. (The War Zone; Air & Space Forces Magazine.)
- ERAM, the Extended Range Attack Munition: where RAACM and Rusty Dagger came from; in production 14 months after first contract. (Air & Space Forces Magazine.)
- Leidos AGM-190A Black Arrow small cruise missile, pallet-launched. (Air & Space Forces Magazine.)
- Lockheed CMMT: CMMT-D is an unpowered glide vehicle expected to cost $150,000. So a prime is in this game too. (Air & Space Forces Magazine.)
- Castelion Blackbeard low-cost hypersonic: already in Episode 3e.

**Barracuda-500 figures (company data).** Range over 500 nautical miles (575 miles); 100-pound payload; speed 190 to 500 knots; variants share more than 90 percent of components. (Air & Space Forces Magazine; The Defense Post.)

**Is Kris's "longer range, less weight" right?** Mostly.
- Range: the Barracuda-500's 500+ nautical miles is well beyond the baseline JASSM and LRASM's publicly cited range, and about level with JASSM-ER. *(JASSM, JASSM-ER and LRASM ranges here are from memory. Check before recording.)*
- Weight: far lighter than a 2,000-pound-class JASSM. *(No confirmed Barracuda-500 weight found yet. Find it.)*
- The caveat: the range comes at low weight because the warhead shrank, about 100 pounds against roughly 1,000 for a JASSM, and the airframe is not a stealth design. It is a design choice, not a propulsion breakthrough.
- Fair framing for air: the new missiles match or beat the old on reach, win on weight and cost, and pay in punch and individual survivability. They win by arriving in numbers. The old ones still do things the cheap ones cannot, such as LRASM picking one ship out of a defended group on its own.

**Author's arithmetic (flag it).** Twelve FAMM rounds at $218,000 is about $2.6 million. Twelve JASSMs at $1.3 million is about $15.6 million. On 3d's $10 million rebuilt 737, the cheap load costs a quarter of the airframe; the JASSM load costs more than the airplane. The whole system becomes attritable, not just the jet.

**Air-to-air — where "cheap" is weakest.**
- Long-range programs are high-end: AIM-260 JATM (framework production agreement September 17, 2026; quantities and cost not disclosed), Navy AIM-174B (air-launched SM-6), Navy AIM-424 Malice (in flight test). (The War Zone; Migflug.)
- AFLRW, the Air Force Long Range Weapon: target range at least 1,000 nautical miles, primarily air-to-air, with an air-to-surface version also wanted. (The War Zone.) **Note for the series:** Episode 2 treats AFLRW as the standoff strike magazine. Reporting describes it as air-to-air first. Kris's air-to-air idea is closer to the program than the strike framing was.
- The low-cost effort is early: an Air Force request for information for a Low-Cost High-Speed Air-to-Air Missile in two sizes, one AMRAAM-sized for maximum range and one half the size of an AMRAAM "to double aircraft loadout." (The War Zone.)

## 2. The dumb truck — what it implies *(inference, following Kris's rule)*

- No radar, no operator stations, no mission crew. That is what keeps the $10 million figure honest. Add sensors and it becomes an aircraft too valuable to risk.
- The one smart thing on board is a datalink: receive a target, pass it to the weapon, release.
- Cruise missiles against fixed targets need coordinates. Air-to-air needs someone else to keep updating the missile in flight, so the truck releases and turns for home while a fighter, a drone or an E-7 guides the shot.
- It matches Episode 2: sensing forward at the island chains, the magazine behind.
- The honest weakness, and the hand-off to Episode 11: a dumb truck is worth exactly as much as the kill chain that tells it where to shoot. If the network is jammed or destroyed, it is a 737 full of missiles with nothing to aim at.
- Fleet-in-being angle: an adversary cannot tell from outside which 737s are loaded.
- The E-7 and the P-8 are both 737s. Sensor, sub-hunter and truck on one airframe family is a legion point, but the truck itself carries no sensors.

## 3. The new wing

**The precedent: the P-8 already has a weapons wing (reported).**
- The P-8 is a 737-800 fuselage with strengthened 737-900-derived wings and raked wingtips in place of winglets. (GlobalSecurity; FlightGlobal; AIN.)
- Four underwing pylons, two per wing, each with a 3,000-pound limit. The internal wing structure was "specially strengthened" for them. (AIN, 2008.)
- Also two centerline hardpoints and a five-station weapons bay, each bay station limited to 1,450 pounds. (AIN; GlobalSecurity.)
- It is assembled on the commercial line at Renton, with the stronger wing mated there. (FlightGlobal.)

So Kris's wing is the P-8 wing taken further: three underwing stations per side where the P-8 has two, plus a tip station.

**The precedent for re-winging an existing fleet: the A-10 (reported).**
- 173 A-10s received new Boeing wing sets under a $1.1 billion program. (Task & Purpose; FlightGlobal.)
- A second contract: up to $999 million for up to 112 wing sets and spare kits; about $240 million for the first 27. (Air & Space Forces Magazine; Defense News.)
- Author's arithmetic: about $6.4 million a set on the first program, about $8.9 million a set on the second.

**The cost problem (inference).** A 737 wing is far larger than an A-10 wing, so a new one would likely cost more than an A-10 set. That means the wing alone could cost more than the $2 million airframe, and possibly more than the whole $10 million rebuild in 3d. The idea may turn a $10 million truck into a $20 to $30 million one. Still far below an $80 million new jet, but 3d's headline number does not survive unchanged. No public figure exists for a 737 wing set; say so on air.

**Why it may still be the better idea (inference).**
- A commercial wing was designed for spread-out lift and an engine pylon, not for point loads hung outboard. 3f found no public cost for adding hardpoints to an existing wing. A wing designed from the start with hardpoints, stores wiring kept out of the fuel, and release clearances is cleaner engineering than cutting into an old one.
- It moves the work from one-at-a-time depot modification to a production line. Wings are built in a factory in quantity and bolted on in a hangar. That is the legion model, and it could shorten 3f's "hangar year."
- A new wing also resets the fatigue life of the part of an old airliner that wears out.

**The wingtip station (inference).**
- Weight at the tip relieves wing bending in flight, the same reason tip tanks work, and fighters such as the F-16 carry tip missiles.
- The risk is flutter. A 737 wing is long and flexible, and a heavy mass at the very tip changes how it twists and bends. Rail launches at the tip also put exhaust and loads next to the ailerons.
- A four-pack of AMRAAM-size missiles is roughly 1,400 pounds plus the launcher. *(AMRAAM weight from memory; check.)* That is a lot for a 737 wingtip.
- The half-size missile in the Air Force's low-cost request fits this station much better: four of them would weigh about what two AMRAAMs do. The tip four-pack is most believable with that missile.
- A tip launcher replaces the P-8's raked tip, so some cruise efficiency is lost.

**Other things to check.**
- Ground clearance: the 737 sits low. Small-diameter cheap missiles help.
- Every new store on every station needs separation and flutter testing. That is the hidden cost of any weapons wing. Standard lugs on the FAMM lugged rounds help.
- Whether the P-8 wing tooling is available for more wings.

**Rough loadout (author's arithmetic).** Three underwing stations a side is six, the same "six pylons" 3f arrives at, plus eight air-to-air missiles on the two tips. With light FAMM-class rounds on multiple racks, the underwing count could be much higher; that needs a real missile weight and a real rack.
