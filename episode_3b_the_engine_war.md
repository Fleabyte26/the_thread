## COLD OPEN

*(An F-16 at altitude. Silence. Then a bang from the back of the jet.)*

In the late 1970s, an F-15 or F-16 pilot at high altitude and high Mach could light the afterburner and, every so often, hear the engine choke.

The afterburner would fail to light, or light and blow out, and the fuel being sprayed into the exhaust would go off all at once. The pressure wave ran forward through the engine and stalled it. On a twin-engine Eagle, that was a bad day. On a single-engine Viper, it was a much worse one.

*(Beat.)*

The Air Force's answer wasn't a better engine. It was a second company. It built a rival from the core of a bomber engine and made the two of them fight for every contract, every year, for almost a decade. It called that a success. Then, thirty years later, when Congress tried to do it again for the F-35, the Pentagon killed it.

And on November 5, 2025, a startup building an AI-piloted fighter announced which engine it had chosen: the one that came out of that fight.

## PART ONE — THE OTHER DOOR

*(Map: the F101 core in the center. Lines branching outward.)*

In 1970 the Air Force picked General Electric's F101 to power the B-1 bomber. The heart of it, the high-pressure core, became famous for leaving the country: shipped to France and kept in a locked room, it became the CFM56 airliner engine. That's another episode.

But the same core also stayed home and became a fighter engine. And that story is a fight.

### The F100 and the stall

Go back to the F-15. When the Eagle entered service, it flew on Pratt & Whitney's F100. On paper the F100 was brilliant. The first version produced nearly 24,000 pounds of thrust in afterburner and hit its target thrust-to-weight ratio of eight, which is what gave the F-15 its startling climb.

In service it had problems. The early F100-PW-100 and -200 suffered stall stagnations and a short life, which meant high maintenance costs. The stall mechanism, as it was understood, went like this: the afterburner would fail to light, or would light and then blow out, and the big jets of fuel being sprayed into the exhaust would ignite all at once, sending a pressure wave forward that stalled the engine. These stalls tended to happen at high Mach and high altitude, and if nobody corrected them they could seriously damage the turbine.

The F-16 entered service on the same engine family. A single-engine fighter with an engine that sometimes stalls is a very different thing from a twin-engine fighter with the same habit.

## PART TWO — THE GREAT ENGINE WAR

So the Air Force did something unusual. It created its own competitor.

It started funding a GE engine called the F101 Derivative Fighter Engine, the bomber core again, as a rival to the F100, partly to put some urgency into Pratt & Whitney. The F101 DFE flew in an F-16 after ground tests finished in 1980 and showed a considerable improvement in performance and operability over the F100 then in service.

In 1983 and 1984 the Air Force launched the Alternate Fighter Engine competition. The Secretary of the Air Force, Verne Orr, called it perhaps the most significant Air Force acquisition initiative in years. Everyone else called it the Great Engine War. From 1984 the Air Force bought both engines, competing the contracts every fiscal year, with the split between Pratt and GE shifting from year to year. The competitions ran until 1992.

The F101 DFE became the F110. Pratt's answer, the F100-PW-220, eliminated almost all of the stall-stagnation and afterburner instability problems of the -100 and doubled the time between depot overhauls. The Navy looked at the Air Force's results and chose the F110 to re-engine the F-14 Tomcat in 1984, which is the engine that finally gave the Tomcat the power its pilots had wanted since 1973. Then, in the mid-1980s, the Air Force ran another round, the Improved Performance Engine program, aiming at the 29,000-pound class. Out came the F100-PW-229 and the F110-GE-129.

*(Beat.)*

The Air Force's own historian of the episode, Colonel Robert Drewes, wrote that the outcome deserved not criticism but praise: a story, as he put it, of government setting things right. Competition worked. Two locked rooms were better than one.

Remember that, because the Pentagon would forget it.

## PART THREE — THE ENGINE THAT WOULDN'T RETIRE

The F110-GE-129 is still in production. It powers F-16s from Block 50 onward, the F-15E, and the newest Eagle, the F-15EX. It's built under license in Japan, Turkey, and South Korea. GE says it has passed eleven million flight hours and forty years of continuous production.

And on November 5, 2025, Shield AI announced that it had picked the F110-GE-129 to power X-BAT.

*(Shield AI X-BAT renders.)*

X-BAT is the AI-piloted, vertical-takeoff jet fighter Shield AI unveiled in Washington in October 2025. Roughly 26 feet long with a 39-foot wingspan, a ceiling near 50,000 feet, more than 2,000 nautical miles of range, and an internal weapons bay. It's designed to operate from ships, trailers, and rough forward strips, flown by Shield AI's Hivemind autonomy software, either alone or as a wingman for crewed jets. CNBC put the price at around $27 million each.

To take off vertically, the F110 will carry GE's Axisymmetric Vectoring Exhaust Nozzle, AVEN, a 3D thrust-vectoring nozzle that GE first tested decades ago on the NF-16D VISTA research jet. Shield AI says the vectoring is used for vertical flight and also for maneuvering in normal flight.

*(On screen, plainly: CORRECTION FROM THE AUTHOR'S NOTES.)*

The author's working notes had X-BAT flying on repurposed old F100s. That's wrong. X-BAT uses the GE F110, the bomber-core engine, and the truth is a better thread than the note. The engine family that came out of the B-1's heart in 1970 is now meant to power an AI fighter in the 2030s.

The F100 does turn up in a repurposing story, just not this one. It's the turbine inside a hypersonic engine, and that's a later episode.

## PART FOUR — THE TF34 AND THE COST OF HURRYING

*(Archival: A-10s. S-3 Vikings on carrier decks.)*

Here is a smaller story about the same industry. It isn't a disaster. It's a small, instructive failure, and it explains why engine programs look slow even when everyone wants them to go fast.

GE's TF34 first ran in 1971 and was developed for the Navy's S-3 Viking, the carrier-based submarine hunter. Fairchild picked it for the Air Force's A-10. The A-10 version first flew in May 1972 and went into production in October 1974.

In 1980 the Comptroller General sent Congress a report on whether the services had fixed their management problems in buying jet engines. The TF34 got its own section. Adapting the Navy engine to fit the A-10 took two years and three months and cost $14.5 million. According to GAO, the development period didn't leave time to fix several engine problems before production, and shortcuts were taken to cut down development testing. Both the Air Force version and the Navy original kept having durability problems. GAO estimated that by 1983 the two services would have spent about $210 million on component improvement programs for the engine.

*(Beat.)*

Do the arithmetic the report invites. Saving time in a $14.5 million development program cost roughly fourteen times that much in fixes afterward.

*(On screen: INFERENCE. The 14x ratio is the author's division of two GAO figures, not a GAO conclusion.)*

And yet, the same engine ended up as one of the quiet successes of the business. In 2009, an Air Force Reserve wing in Missouri pulled a TF34 off an A-10 after ten years on the same aircraft and more than 3,400 hours since its last overhaul. It came off because of a time-compliance order, not because anything was wrong. The engine people there said ten years was a record for the wing.

The TF34 also had a civilian child, the CF34, which powers regional and business jets around the world, including COMAC's C909 regional jet in China. In 2025, when Washington briefly cut off engine exports to China's COMAC, the CF34 for the C909 was on the list, right beside the LEAP for the C919. The TF34's grandchild turned out to be a lever too.

## PART FIVE — THE SECOND ENGINE WAR, CANCELLED

*(Archival: F-35 first flights. Congressional hearings, 2009–2011.)*

When the Joint Strike Fighter came along, Congress remembered the Great Engine War.

In fiscal year 1996, Congress required the Pentagon to develop an alternate engine for the F-35. The F-35 flew on Pratt & Whitney's F135, a derivative of the F119 from the F-22. The alternate became the F136, built by GE with Rolls-Royce and derived from the YF120, the engine that had lost the F-22 competition. GE held sixty percent and Rolls-Royce forty.

It was the 1980s logic again: two engines, two companies, competition every year.

The Pentagon didn't want it. From FY2007 through FY2010, the administration tried to kill it and Congress refused. Defense Secretary Gates called it the extra engine. In FY2011, Congress agreed not to fund it. On April 25, 2011, the Defense Department terminated the contract and ordered the tooling and the engines handed back to the government.

GE and Rolls-Royce offered to keep developing the engine with their own money. GAO found that the self-funding offer could save $700 million through fiscal 2012. On December 2, 2011, the two companies announced they were stopping anyway.

According to the Congressional Research Service, the GE/Rolls team had received $2.4 billion between FY1995 and FY2009.

*(Beat.)*

So the second engine war ended before it started. Today the F-35 has one engine supplier. Pratt & Whitney says it has delivered more than 1,500 F135 production engines to twenty allied nations.

*(On screen: INFERENCE.)* Whether the F136 would have saved money over the life of the program is still argued, and the arguments on both sides are made by people with a stake in the answer. What's not in dispute is that the 1980s competition existed, worked, and was cited as a model by the Air Force's own historian, and that the Pentagon chose not to repeat it.

## CLOSE — TWO KEYS

*(Two engines side by side on stands. One dark. One running.)*

In the 1980s, the Air Force learned something it wrote down and praised: two suppliers fighting for every order produced better engines, faster, than one supplier with a guaranteed customer. The F100-PW-220 and the F110 were proof.

In the 1970s, the TF34 showed the opposite lesson: hurry the development, skip the testing, and pay for it many times over in fixes afterward.

In 2011 the Pentagon decided it didn't want a second key. The F-35 has one engine maker. And the most interesting new airplane to come out of an American startup, X-BAT, is going to fly on an engine from 1980s production, because that's the engine the competition made good.

*(Beat.)*

The F-47's engine is now a contest again, GE against Pratt & Whitney, two adaptive engines. Same logic as the Great Engine War, new name. Whether that's lesson learned or lesson rediscovered, the episode doesn't know.
