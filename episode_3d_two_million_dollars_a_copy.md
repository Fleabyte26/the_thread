# THE THREAD — Episode 3d: Two Million Dollars a Copy

**Status:** Draft 1, long cut
**Working title:** "Two Million Dollars a Copy" (alternative: "The Birds in the Desert")
**Format:** Third-person narration throughout. No first-person breaks.
**Register:** Inquisitive. It wanders. It argues with an earlier episode, out loud, because that's what the thread does.
**Sourcing discipline:** Facts stated as facts. ## COLD OPEN

*(Wide aerial: rows of airliners in the desert outside Victorville, California. Heat shimmer. No music.)*

There are places in the American desert where airliners go to wait.

Victorville. Mojave. Roswell. Marana. Rows of them, wingtip to wingtip, engines bagged, windows taped, tails still painted in the colors of airlines that have moved on to newer jets.

Some of them will fly again. Most won't. The ones that won't are worth more in pieces than whole. The engines get pulled and sold. The landing gear, the avionics, the galleys and the seats go to brokers. What's left is aluminum.

*(Beat.)*

At the end of its life, a twenty-year-old Boeing 737-800, the most common jetliner of its generation, can be bought for something like two million dollars.

A new 737 off the line in Renton costs tens of millions. List prices run past a hundred million.

In Episode 2, this series built a plan around the 737. A reserve of arsenal jets, each carrying a magazine of long-range missiles, standing behind cheap drones on the Pacific island chains. And it assumed those jets would be new.

This episode says that assumption was wrong. And the reason it was wrong goes back three hundred and thirty-six years, to an English admiral who didn't want to fight.

---

## PART ONE — THE FLEET THAT EXISTED

*(Period paintings: the Battle of Beachy Head, 1690. The English Channel. Sails.)*

In the summer of 1690, England was at war with France, and the French fleet in the Channel was stronger than the English one.

The English commander, Arthur Herbert, Earl of Torrington, didn't want to give battle on bad terms. His reasoning, as it has come down to us, was that as long as the English had a fleet in being, the French wouldn't dare attempt an invasion. Keep the fleet intact. Keep it somewhere the French have to worry about. Make them count it.

His government didn't trust the idea. Queen Mary's council ordered him to fight. He fought at Beachy Head, and he lost. He was court-martialed, acquitted, and never commanded at sea again.

*(Beat.)*

And then the strange part. The French won the battle and didn't exploit it. There was no invasion. The English fleet, beaten and battered, still existed, and the French behaved as if it mattered.

Torrington was right, and his own side punished him for it.

The phrase "fleet in being" is his. But the doctrine, the idea worked out as strategy, came two centuries later. In 1891 a Royal Navy officer, Philip Colomb, went back to Torrington's words and built a theory on them. Alfred Thayer Mahan, the American who wrote the book on sea power, was skeptical. He thought a weaker fleet's deterrent value was overrated. Julian Corbett, the British strategist, was more sympathetic. The argument has never fully closed.

The cleanest modern example came in the First World War. Germany's High Seas Fleet spent most of the war in port. It fought one great battle, at Jutland in 1916, and then mostly stayed home. But because it existed, the British Grand Fleet had to stay concentrated in the North Sea for four years, tied down by a fleet that rarely sailed.

*(On screen: one line.)* A fleet in being deters by existing and being counted.

That's the whole idea this episode stands on. Hold on to it.

## PART TWO — WHAT EPISODE 2 GOT WRONG

*(The Episode 2 map: island chains, drones forward, arsenal jets behind. Then a red mark on one assumption.)*

*(Banner: INFERENCE · THE AUTHOR'S PLAN.)*

Episode 2 put together a plan from real pieces. The Air Force's Rapid Dragon program had shown that ordinary cargo aircraft can launch long-range cruise missiles from pallets. The P-8 Poseidon had shown that a 737 can be turned into a warplane without stopping the commercial line. The X-68A LongShot and the AFLRW program pointed toward long-range weapons that could be launched from far behind the front. Put those together and you get a reserve of 737-based arsenal jets, each carrying something like a dozen long-range missiles, standing behind drones on the island chains.

To be clear again, as Episode 2 was: nobody in the Pentagon has proposed this. It's the author's synthesis, built from real parts.

But in the episode's notes, one assumption slid in without much argument. The airframes, it said, would most plausibly come from new production at Renton, rather than from reactivated old civilian jets. And it admitted it hadn't checked how many old jets there were.

*(Beat.)*

Think about what that assumption actually means. The plan is a deterrent. Its job is to exist and be counted. If a war ever came, its active life might be a year, maybe less. Most of its life would be spent parked in a desert, preserved, waiting.

So the plan, as written, would buy brand-new jets at full price, in a market where airlines are already waiting years for deliveries, and park them in the sand.

Why would anyone build white tails at eighty million dollars a copy to store in the desert, when the desert is already full of birds?

---

## PART THREE — THE BIRDS ALREADY IN THE DESERT

*(Victorville, Mojave, Roswell, Marana. Satellite views. Rows of 737s.)*

Start with how many there are.

Boeing built 4,991 commercial 737-800s. It's by far the most numerous version of the 737 Next Generation. The 737-800 was the default narrowbody of the 2000s and 2010s, flown by almost every airline that flew Boeings.

Now those jets are reaching the age when airlines let them go. Some get a second life as freighters: Boeing passed its 200th 737-800 freighter conversion around a decade after launching the program, which is only about four percent of everything it built. Some keep flying passengers a little longer. And a growing share go to the desert to be taken apart.

*(Beat.)*

And here's the detail that changes the plan from a fantasy into something with an engineering record behind it.

The P-8 Poseidon, the Navy's submarine hunter, the aircraft Episode 2 held up as proof that a 737 can be a warplane, is built on the 737-800.

The Navy and Boeing have already done the hard work of turning this exact airframe into a military aircraft: a weapons bay, wing hardpoints, military certification, a maintenance system, a training pipeline. It's the airframe sitting in the desert.

*(On screen: P-8 profile, then a 737-800 profile, overlaid. They match.)*

---

## PART FOUR — WHAT A BIRD ACTUALLY COSTS

*(On screen: a simple ladder of prices. Banner: market ranges, trade sources.)*

Now the money, carefully, because aircraft prices are slippery.

A new 737 has a list price that has run past a hundred million dollars, though very few airlines pay list. New 737 MAX jets actually change hands in the high tens of millions.

A used 737-800 in ordinary airline condition sells for roughly fifteen to thirty million dollars, depending on age and condition.

But an old 737-800 at the end of its life is a different object. On a jet like that, nearly all the value sits in the two engines under the wings. The airframe itself is close to scrap. A twenty-two-year-old -800 coming up on its next heavy check can be worth less as a flying aircraft than the sum of its parts.

So the price depends almost entirely on the engines.

A healthy CFM56-7B, the engine on every 737 Next Generation, is worth somewhere around three million dollars, more for one with plenty of life left. IBA's half-life figures run from about $3.8 million to $7.1 million. Put two of those on the wings and you have a valuable airplane.

But an engine near the end of its life is worth much less. Sold as a teardown core, to be taken apart for used parts, a CFM56-7B goes for somewhere around half a million to a million dollars.

So a tired 737-800 in the desert, headed for part-out, with engines near the end of their life, can be had for something like two million dollars.

*(Beat.)*

And now look at what that means for this plan.

---

## PART FIVE — THE ONE-YEAR ENGINE

*(Banner: INFERENCE · THE AUTHOR'S PLAN.)*

An airline pays a premium for engine life, because it plans to fly that engine for years: thousands of flights, carrying passengers, every day.

This plan doesn't need that.

If the arsenal jet's active mission is a year at most, it doesn't need an engine with ten years of life left. It needs an engine that will run reliably for a few hundred missions. An engine with a few thousand cycles left on its life-limited parts is more than enough.

Those are the engines the market is selling for teardown, because they're worth little to an airline.

*(Beat.)*

So the plan inverts the market. The thing that makes these birds nearly worthless to an airline, not much life left, is exactly what makes them cheap and good enough for a deterrent. The plan buys what everyone else is throwing away.

Now the rough arithmetic, flagged as the author's:

*(On screen, the table:)*

| Item | Rough figure | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| End-of-life 737-800 airframe | ~$2M | part-out market, trade sources |
| Two low-life CFM56-7Bs (if bought separately) | ~$1–2M | teardown-core values |
| Weapons conversion | **unknown** | no public figure; likely the biggest line |
| **Target all-in** | **~$10M** | the author's estimate |

The weapons conversion is the honest gap. Putting hardpoints or a weapons bay on a 737 is real structural work, much closer to what was done for the P-8 than to a freighter conversion. Nobody has published what it would cost on an old airframe. Ten million dollars all-in is a target, not a sourced number.

But follow the logic anyway. Even if the real number were two or three times that, it would still be a fraction of a new jet.

And then the comparison that matters. The money for ten new white tails at eighty million each, eight hundred million dollars, buys something like eighty rebuilt old birds at ten million.

Ten jets in the desert, or eighty.

*(Beat.)*

If the whole point is to be counted, that's not a small difference. It's the difference between a plan the other side can dismiss and a plan the other side has to plan around.

*(Beat.)*

Or put it the way a buyer would, walking the rows at Victorville. The desert is a flea market. I'll give you two million a copy for all hundred and thirty.

A hundred and thirty birds at two million dollars each is two hundred and sixty million dollars. That's about the price of three new white tails.

Three jets the other side can shrug off. Or a hundred and thirty it has to count.

## PART SIX — THE ENGINE THAT ISN'T SCARCE

*(Renton line footage. Then CFM56 engines on stands, rows of them.)*

There's a second reason the old birds are better, and it's about engines again. The thread keeps coming back to them.

Every new 737 MAX flies on a CFM LEAP engine. And the LEAP is the bottleneck of the entire narrowbody world. CFM delivered 1,802 LEAPs in 2025 and expects about fifteen percent more in 2026, and its parent company has said it won't deliver more in 2026 than it has already committed. Airbus deliveries slowed in early 2026 with engine shortages named as the main cause.

So a plan that buys new 737s for the desert is a plan that takes LEAP engines away from airlines that are already waiting for them, and production slots away from a factory line that is already the most watched in the industry. It would fight the commercial world for the scarcest thing in it.

The old 737-800 flies on the CFM56: the engine from the locked room in Episode 3a, the best-selling jet engine in history. About 14,200 CFM56 engines are still active, and more than 2,500 are already in storage or teardown. The spares market is deep, the shops know it, and the parts exist all over the world.

*(Beat.)*

The new-build plan competes for the bottleneck. The old-bird plan lives on the surplus.

---

## PART SEVEN — THE GOVERNMENT HAS DONE THIS BEFORE

*(AMARG, Davis-Monthan. KC-135 rows. Tinker AFB.)*

It's also worth noticing that the U.S. government already knows how to keep jets in the desert on purpose.

Episode 2 told this story. When the Air Force retired its KC-135E tankers, Congress required that a number of them be kept in a protected storage status rather than scrapped. The Air Force later asked to be allowed to let them go. And in April 2026, the Air Force pulled KC-135s out of storage at the boneyard and flew them back into service, because it needed tankers faster than new ones could arrive.

*(On screen: recheck the KC-135E storage figures and the April 2026 regeneration against the Episode 2 sources before recording.)*

So the pieces all exist. A storage site. A preservation process. A legal mechanism for keeping jets that would otherwise be scrapped. A recent example of pulling them back out when they were needed.

And storage is cheap. In 2019, the base fee at Victorville for storing a 737 was reported at about four hundred and fifty dollars a month, before maintenance and preservation work.

Four hundred and fifty dollars a month to keep a piece of the deterrent in the sand.

---

## PART EIGHT — WHAT DETERS

*(Back to Beachy Head. Then a map of the western Pacific.)*

Now go back to Torrington.

His fleet wasn't the newest or the strongest in the Channel. It lost the battle it was forced into. What made it work was that it existed and the French had to account for it.

A deterrent isn't judged on its specifications. It's judged on whether the other side believes it can be done: quickly, in numbers, and with what's at hand.

*(Banner: INFERENCE · THE AUTHOR'S PLAN.)*

Measured that way, the old-bird plan is more credible than the new-build one, not less:

- It can be done, because the P-8 already turned this airframe into a warplane.
- It can be done fast, because the donor jets are already parked, by the thousands, in the desert and in airline fleets around the world, and nobody has to wait for a factory slot.
- It can be done at scale, because the engines are the surplus, not the bottleneck, and because each bird is cheap enough to buy dozens.

*(Beat.)*

And that leads to the strangest idea in this episode, the one closest to Torrington's. The plan itself may be the deterrent.

If the other side knows the donor pool exists, knows the conversion has been engineered, knows the missiles are in production and that a reserve of old jets could be stood up in months, then the plan does part of its work before a single aircraft is converted. Like Torrington's fleet, it shapes the other side's calculations by existing as a possibility they have to count.

That isn't a free lunch. A threat has to be believable, so some jets have to actually exist, and some have to fly and fire in exercises where they can be seen. But a cheap plan can afford to be visible. An expensive one tends to stay small.

## PART NINE — THE OTHER SIDE OF THE LEDGER

*(Plain. A list on screen.)*

The series doesn't let a good argument go unchallenged, including its own. So here's the case against.

The conversion is the unknown. A weapons-capable 737 is not a freighter. The P-8 was a full military development program. Doing a cheaper version on old airframes might cost much more than ten million dollars a copy, and nobody has published a number.

Rapid Dragon needs a ramp. The pallet system that inspired this plan rolls missiles out of the back of a C-17 or C-130. A 737 has no rear ramp. An arsenal 737 would have to launch from a weapons bay or from wing pylons, and which works best is an open engineering question. That was true in Episode 2 with new jets too, so it doesn't favor new over old, but it hasn't gone away.

Old jets aren't free to keep. Corrosion, inspections, records, crews, maintenance training. A reserve that's actually ready costs more than a parking fee.

The market would notice. Every flea-market haggler knows the catch in "all hundred and thirty." The moment you say all of them, the seller knows you want all of them. One bird at a time, quietly, the price holds. Announce that you're buying the whole desert, and every broker with a CFM56 on a stand raises his price overnight. And CFM56 values are already rising, because the LEAP shortage is keeping old 737s flying longer. A government buying hundreds of donor jets would be competing with airlines and part-out shops for the same engines. A plan like this would work best bought quietly and steadily, ten birds a quarter for three years. That happens to fit a deterrent that is meant to sit in the desert being counted.

And the missiles cost more than the trucks. A dozen long-range missiles per jet is the expensive part of every version of this plan. The case for cheap airframes is partly that you shouldn't spend the money on the truck when the magazine is where the money has to go.

*(Beat.)*

And finally: China can do the same thing. It has its own stored airliners, its own freighters, its own long-range missiles. Episode 11 called this the symmetry trap. Any cheap, clever idea is cheap and clever for both sides.

---

## CLOSE — THE BIRDS IN THE DESERT

*(The Victorville rows at sunset. Long shadows under the wings.)*

So here is where the thread has ended up, a little different from where Episode 2 left it.

The arsenal plan doesn't need new jets. It needs old ones, the cheaper and more tired the better, as long as they can fly for a year. It needs the engines nobody else wants. It needs a desert, which the country has plenty of, and a storage fee that rounds to nothing. And it needs the other side to know all of that.

Torrington lost a battle and kept a fleet, and the French behaved as if the fleet mattered. Three centuries later the same logic may be sitting in the sand outside Victorville, in rows, with bagged engines and faded airline paint, at something like two million dollars a copy.

*(Beat. A covered market: stalls, lamps, a merchant behind a counter. Then a card table.)*

Maybe the lesson isn't really about airplanes. Maybe it's about two old ways of doing business.

The first is the souk, the old Arab market. The merchant who does well never shows how much he wants something. He's patient. He comes back next week. He keeps more than one seller in play. And he can always walk away, which is the strongest move in the market.

*(Banner: INTERPRETATION · ONE READING.)*

American defense procurement, the series has argued for three episodes now, tends to do the opposite. It publishes exactly what it wants, years in advance. It buys from single suppliers, so there's nobody to walk away to. It signs contracts where the seller has little reason to haggle. And it treats walking away as failure. The Constellation was rare because, for once, the Navy walked away. The F136 in Episode 3b was the opposite: the Pentagon gave up its second seller on purpose.

The second is the card table. The bluff is the part of poker people remember, but serious players know a pure bluff gets called eventually. What wins is the semi-bluff: a bet made with a hand that isn't strong yet but could become strong on the next card. The other player has to respect it, because if he calls, the hand might turn real.

That's what the birds in the desert are. A pure bluff would be a plan on paper with nothing behind it. A hundred and thirty jets bought at two million dollars a copy, with a known conversion path and missiles in production, are a semi-bluff. They aren't yet a force, but anyone counting them knows they could become one in months. Torrington, in 1690, was making a semi-bluff. His fleet wasn't strong enough to win. It was real enough that France couldn't ignore it.

And the honest limit, which any card player knows too: you have to show real cards sometimes, or the table learns you're always bluffing. For this plan, that means a few birds actually converted, actually flying and actually firing in an exercise the other side can watch.

Buy like the merchant in the souk. Fight like the player at the table. And show your cards just often enough.

Is it a good idea? Is it already in a classified binder? Would the conversion cost blow the whole thing up? The episode doesn't know.

But it knows the birds are there.
