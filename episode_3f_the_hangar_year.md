# THE THREAD — Episode 3f: The Hangar Year

**Status:** Draft 1, long cut
**Working title:** "The Hangar Year" (alternative: "A Bay, Six Pylons and a Hangar")
**Format:** Third-person narration throughout. No first-person breaks.
**Register:** Inquisitive and practical. Less history, more wrench.
**Sourcing discipline:** Facts stated as facts. Where no public figure exists (the cost of a weapons-bay retrofit, the cost of new wing hardpoints), the episode says so. The engineering reasoning about retrofits is flagged INFERENCE · AUTHOR'S ARITHMETIC. The plan itself is flagged INFERENCE · THE AUTHOR'S PLAN.
**Note:** Episode 3e pointed toward solid rocket motors. The thread turned back instead, to the question Episode 3d left open: what it actually takes to put weapons on a 737.

---

## COLD OPEN

*(A 737-800 in a hangar, cowlings open, seats gone. Work lights. No music.)*

From fifty feet away, a Navy P-8 Poseidon and an airline 737-800 look like the same airplane. Same fuselage. Same engines. Same cockpit, more or less. You've probably flown on one of them.

They are not the same airplane.

Episode 3d argued that the arsenal jet in Episode 2 shouldn't be new. It should be an old 737-800 bought out of the desert for something like two million dollars, rebuilt for about ten, and kept as a deterrent. It said the hard part was the conversion, and it said the conversion cost was unknown.

Episode 3e was supposed to lead to solid rocket motors. The thread turned back instead. Because 3d's unknown is the whole plan.

So this episode goes into the hangar. What would it actually take to give an old airliner a weapons bay like the P-8's? To hang six missiles under it? And who in the world already does this kind of work, taking airplanes other people have written off and sending them back out nearly new?

*(Beat.)*

The answer starts with a surprise. The P-8 was never a converted airliner. It was born different.

---

## PART ONE — WHAT THE P-8 ACTUALLY IS

Boeing calls the P-8's basis the 737-800ERX. The fuselage comes from Spirit AeroSystems in Wichita, which builds every 737 fuselage. But the P-8 fuselage isn't the airline one. It uses thicker aluminum skin, and its structure is strengthened throughout, floors included, for flying low over the sea and turning hard.

The wing isn't the 737-800's either. It's derived from the longer 737-900, strengthened inside, with raked tips instead of winglets. Under it are four weapons pylons, each rated, according to Boeing engineers in 2008, at 3,000 pounds.

Under the fuselage there are two more hardpoints on the centerline. And behind the wing, in the belly, there's a weapons bay with five stations for torpedoes, mines and depth charges.

That bay was not cut into a finished airplane. Spirit added a separate sub-assembly position on the line, so the weapons bay and an auxiliary fuel tank could be built into the fuselage before it was joined up. The P-8 is built with its bay, the way a car is built with its trunk.

It also carries about three times the wiring of a standard 737, more cooling ducts, nearly a hundred antennas, and an air-refueling receptacle. Its maximum takeoff weight is 189,200 pounds. An airline 737-800's is 174,200. Boeing has said the two share about 60 percent of their parts.

*(Beat.)*

So the honest description of the P-8 is a military airplane that shares a family resemblance and a production line with an airliner. That's why it cost what it cost. The Navy's 2022 budget put the flyaway cost at $173 million each.

And one more fact belongs here. The Navy's 2027 budget request asks for twelve more P-8s and calls 2027 the last planned year of production. The final delivery is expected in early fiscal 2032. After that, the line that knows how to build the bay goes quiet.

---

## PART TWO — WHY A BAY IS HARD TO ADD

Now the problem. A 737-800 coming out of the desert has a fuselage that was built as a pressure vessel. Every flight, the cabin is pumped up and let down. A twenty-year-old airliner may have been through tens of thousands of those cycles.

The P-8's bay sits where an airliner has its aft cargo hold, behind the wing, in the belly. On an airliner that area is inside the pressurized hull. To put a bay there on a used jet, you'd have to cut a large opening in the bottom of an old pressure vessel, carry the loads around it, close the cabin off above it with a new pressure floor, and add doors, hinges, actuators, heating and racks for the stores.

That's not a bolt-on. That's surgery on the part of the airplane that keeps it in one piece.

Here's what the thread can find, and what it can't. It can't find any public record of anyone retrofitting a P-8-style weapons bay into a used commercial 737. The bay exists only in airplanes built that way from the start. That doesn't make it impossible. It means the first one would be a development program, with engineering, a test article, fatigue analysis and flight testing, before anyone could quote a price per airplane.

*(Beat.)*

So if the plan in 3d depends on a weapons bay, the "unknown" in its cost table is a large unknown. And it raises a better question. Does the arsenal jet need a bay at all?

---

## PART THREE — THE FREIGHTER PRECEDENT

Before answering that, there's a whole industry that already cuts holes in old 737-800s for a living.

When a 737-800 gets old for passenger flying, many of the best ones become freighters. The conversion cuts a large cargo door into the side of the fuselage, reinforces the floor, removes the windows and seats, adds a barrier wall behind the cockpit and changes the systems for cargo. Converters describe roughly 800 separate tasks for a narrowbody like this, and more for a widebody.

It takes about 90 to 130 days. Analysts and conversion shops put the cost of a 737-800 conversion at roughly four to six million dollars, often done alongside a heavy maintenance check.

Three programs are certified for the 737-800. Boeing's own, the BCF. One by Aeronautical Engineers in Miami, the SF. And one by Israel Aerospace Industries, through its Bedek division, the BDSF, certified by the FAA in 2020.

*(Beat.)*

So the world already knows how to take a used 737-800, cut a large opening in its pressure hull, strengthen around it, certify the result and send it back to work for another fifteen or twenty years. That's not a weapons bay. A side cargo door isn't a hole in the keel. But it's the same kind of work, done in the same kind of hangar, by the same kind of people, at the same airframe age.

That's the most encouraging fact in this episode.

---

## PART FOUR — SIX MISSILES

Now the wings.

The missile the arsenal jet would most likely carry is something like the AGM-158C LRASM, the long-range anti-ship missile, or its land-attack cousin JASSM. Published weights for LRASM run from about 2,100 to about 2,760 pounds, depending on the source. The Navy has paid around three million dollars each.

Six of them is somewhere between thirteen and seventeen thousand pounds, hung on the airplane. And about nineteen million dollars of missiles on a bird the plan hopes to rebuild for ten. As 3d said, the magazine is where the money goes.

Where would six go? Here the P-8 offers a neat answer. It has exactly six external stations: four pylons on the wings and two on the centerline. What the public record doesn't show is whether the centerline stations are cleared for a missile that size. The wing pylons are. In 2025 the Navy released the first official image of a P-8 flying with an LRASM under its wing.

And that image is the sobering part. The Navy started asking industry about putting LRASM on the P-8 in 2020. Fit checks happened that July. The first official flight image came five years later, with testing still under way. That's five years to integrate one missile on an airplane that was designed from the start to carry missiles under its wings.

*(Beat.)*

A desert 737-800 has no hardpoints at all. Its wing was built to carry fuel, flaps and two engines, not 3,000-pound stores hanging off fittings in the wing box. Adding pylons means new structure inside the wing, new wiring out to each station, a stores management system, and then testing that each missile falls away cleanly and doesn't make the wing flutter.

None of that is impossible. All of it is engineering, and all of it has to be done once before it can be done many times.

---

## PART FIVE — MAYBE SKIP THE BAY

Put Parts Two and Four together and a simpler plan falls out.

The P-8's bay is for torpedoes, mines and depth charges: weapons for hunting submarines that drop from close range. An arsenal jet in the 3d plan isn't hunting submarines. It stands far back and launches long-range missiles. Those go on pylons.

So the arsenal jet may not need a bay at all. Leave the pressure hull alone. Put the money into the wing.

That turns the conversion into three jobs instead of four. One: heavy maintenance and structural inspection, the work freighter converters already do. Two: wing hardpoints and wiring, designed once and installed on every bird. Three: a cockpit and mission system good enough to receive a target and launch, not a full P-8 sensor suite. The targeting can come from someone else: a drone, a satellite, a P-8 nearby.

How many missiles? Four on the wings is the P-8's proven layout. Six needs the two centerline stations, or a third pylon on each wing, which would be new engineering on an old wing. A plan that starts with four and grows to six is easier to believe than one that starts at six.

*(Beat.)*

It also changes the price. The freighter world says cutting a cargo door and refitting an old 737-800 costs four to six million dollars. A pylon-only conversion is a different job, but it's a job of that kind. If the first-article engineering is paid once, a per-airplane cost in single-digit millions isn't crazy. The 3d target of ten million all-in would still be ambitious. It wouldn't be absurd.

That's the author's arithmetic, not a quote. Nobody has priced this airplane.

---

## PART SIX — THE HANGAR IN ISRAEL

Who does this kind of work as a habit?

Israel Aerospace Industries, through the division that used to be called Bedek, has made a business out of airplanes other people are finished with. It says it has converted more than 260 airliners to freighters over forty years, and upgraded more than 2,500 military aircraft and helicopters, including F-15s, F-16s, F-4s and Mirages.

Around 2010 it took a used Boeing 767, a passenger jet, and turned it into an aerial tanker, the Multi Mission Tanker Transport, for the Colombian Air Force. The project took about two years. Bedek had done the same thing earlier with old Boeing 707s for Israel's own air force. Find a cheap used airframe, do the cargo conversion, add the refueling gear. The pitch is a tanker for a fraction of a new one.

*(Beat.)*

And then there's the story people usually tell about Israel and the F-15, which is close to true and worth getting exactly right.

In 2016, nine two-seat F-15Ds from the Oregon Air National Guard's 173rd Fighter Wing went to Israel under an American aid package. The Guard had retired them. They were supposed to be used for spare parts.

Israel didn't use them for parts. The Israeli Air Force's own maintenance squadron at Tel Nof air base took them apart, worked out decades of undocumented changes, and rebuilt each one into the Israeli "Baz" configuration, with new weapons and systems. Each jet took about 170 days. The officer in charge said a project like it had never been done before, so they brought in the people who were the air force's living memory of the Baz.

So it wasn't IAI, and it wasn't quite a year. It was an air force depot, about half a year per jet. But the idea is the same: an airplane someone else had written off goes into a hangar and comes out a frontline aircraft.

For scale: in December 2025, the Pentagon awarded Boeing $8.58 billion to build 25 new F-15IA fighters for Israel. That's about $343 million per airplane, including more than the airplane. Israel is buying new F-15s too. It also kept the old ones flying.

## PART SEVEN — WHAT IT WOULD TAKE

So what would the American version of the hangar year need?

**A design authority.** Somebody has to own the engineering for the pylons and the wiring. Boeing has the P-8 data. A freighter converter, or a company like IAI, has the know-how for old 737 structures. The government would have to decide who owns the drawings.

**A first article.** One airplane, converted, instrumented and tested: structure, flutter, missile separation, the stores management software. Based on how long LRASM took on the P-8, that's years, not months. That's the expensive part, paid once.

**A certification path.** Freighter conversions are certified by the FAA. An armed airplane isn't certified that way. It goes through the military's airworthiness process, which can be faster or slower depending on how much the customer wants it.

**Conversion lines.** If each bird takes something like a freighter conversion, 90 to 130 days, then one hangar line might turn out three airplanes a year. A hundred and thirty birds would need many lines, or many years. That's where the hangar year becomes a hangar decade, unless the work is spread out.

**A target.** The arsenal jet can only shoot what someone else finds for it. That part has to exist already.

*(Beat.)*

And it would take a decision that doesn't fit the Pentagon's habits: to treat the first airplane as the hard part and every one after it as a copy. That's how freighter converters think. It's how the Israeli depot thought about the F-15s. It's not how big programs usually think.

---

## PART EIGHT — THE OTHER SIDE OF THE LEDGER

Now the case against.

**Boeing owns the data.** The P-8's engineering, and much of the 737's, belongs to Boeing. A plan that copies the P-8's wing hardpoints onto old airliners would need Boeing's cooperation, or would have to redo the engineering from scratch. Boeing would rather sell P-8s.

**The skills leave with the line.** When P-8 production ends, the people who know the bay and the pylons move on. A plan that waits too long has to learn it all again.

**Old wings are old.** A 737-800 from the desert has a fatigue history. Adding 3,000-pound stores to a wing that was never built for them, and then flying it hard, uses up life fast. For a one-year mission, maybe that's fine. For a deterrent that has to stay credible for fifteen years in storage and then fly, it has to be checked bird by bird.

**An airliner is not survivable.** No converted 737 survives being caught by a fighter or a surface-to-air missile. The whole concept depends on launching from far enough away, behind the drones.

**Israel is not America.** Israel's model works for a small fleet, a short supply chain, and an air force that keeps its own institutional memory. American depots are bigger, slower and more contractor-run. The hangar year in Tel Nof may not translate to a hangar year in Oklahoma.

**And the missiles are still the cost.** Nineteen million dollars of missiles on a ten-million-dollar bird. If the magazine isn't funded, the conversion doesn't matter.

---

## CLOSE — A YEAR IN THE HANGAR

*(Back to the hangar. The cowlings closed. A pylon on a stand next to the wing.)*

So go back to the two airplanes that look the same from fifty feet away.

One was built with its weapons inside it, in a factory line that will close in a few years. The other sits in the desert waiting to be parted out.

The gap between them isn't as wide as the P-8's price suggests, and it isn't as narrow as the plan in 3d hoped. A bay is surgery on the pressure hull, and nobody has done it to a used jet. Wing pylons are hard engineering, done once. The rest, the heavy check, the wiring, the paperwork, is work an industry already does every day for freighters.

And somewhere there's a hangar culture that makes this normal: airplanes other people have written off go in, and come out flying. In Israel it's an air force depot and a company that turns old airliners into tankers. In America it exists in the freighter shops, mostly building airplanes to carry packages.

Whether the American government would ever pay those shops to build a deterrent, the episode doesn't know. That's the honest ending.

But it knows the hard part is the first one.
